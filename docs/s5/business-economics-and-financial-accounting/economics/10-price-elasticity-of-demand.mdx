Price elasticity of demand (PED) measures the responsiveness of
quantity demanded to a change in price.

$$
E_d = \frac{\%\text{ change in quantity demanded}}{\%\text{ change in price}}
$$

For discrete changes, the midpoint (arc elasticity) formula is used:

$$
\%\Delta Q
=
\frac{Q_2 - Q_1}{(Q_2 + Q_1)/2},
\quad
\%\Delta P
=
\frac{P_2 - P_1}{(P_2 + P_1)/2}
$$

Because price and quantity demanded are inversely related, price
elasticity of demand has a negative sign. It is commonly expressed
using its absolute value.

## Categories

### Perfectly Inelastic Demand

$E_d = 0$. Quantity demanded does not respond to price.

Examples:

- Insulin for diabetics  
  Quantity demanded is unchanged regardless of price.
- Emergency heart surgery  
  Patients pay any price; no substitutes exist.
- Salt in very small quantities  
  Negligible budget share, no substitute.

### Inelastic Demand

$\lvert E_d\rvert < 1$. Quantity demanded changes by a smaller percentage than price.

Examples:

- Gasoline (short run)  
  Commuters must buy; few immediate alternatives.
- Prescription medications  
  Health necessity, no close substitutes.
- Electricity and water  
  Essential utilities with no alternatives.
- Cigarettes  
  Addictive, few substitutes for smokers.

### Unit Elastic Demand

$\lvert E_d\rvert = 1$. Quantity demanded changes by the same percentage as price.

Examples:

- Certain clothing brands  
  A 10% price rise leads to a 10% quantity fall; total revenue falls slightly (maximized at unit elasticity).
- Mid-range consumer electronics  
  Proportional response keeps total revenue near its maximum.

### Elastic Demand

$\lvert E_d\rvert > 1$. Quantity demanded changes by a larger percentage than price.

Examples:

- Restaurant meals  
  Many substitutes (cooking at home, other restaurants).
- Air travel (leisure)  
  Discretionary, price-sensitive, many alternatives.
- Branded snacks  
  Close substitutes (generic brands, other snacks).
- New cars  
  Large budget share, can delay purchase, many models.

### Perfectly Elastic Demand

$E_d = \infty$. A very small price change causes a very large change in quantity demanded.

Examples:

- Individual wheat farmer in a perfectly competitive market  
  Any price above market clears zero demand.
- Foreign currency exchange at posted rate  
  Buy unlimited at market rate, none above it.
- Shares of a large-cap stock  
  [Horizontal demand](/s5/business-economics-and-financial-accounting/economics/demand#horizontal-demand-curve) at current market price.

## Factors of Price Elasticity

### Factors Increasing Elasticity

Demand tends to be more elastic when:

- Many close substitutes are available.
- The good takes up a large share of income.
- The good is a luxury or non-essential.
- Consumers have a longer time to adjust.
- The market is narrowly defined.

### Factors Increasing Inelasticity

Demand tends to be more inelastic when:

- Few or no close substitutes are available.
- The good takes up a small share of income.
- The good is a necessity or essential.
- Consumers have little time to adjust.
- The market is broadly defined.

## Total Revenue

Total revenue is the amount paid by buyers and received by sellers.

$$
TR = P \times Q
$$

Where:

<Symbols>
  <Sym s="TR">Total Revenue</Sym>
  <Sym s="P">Price</Sym>
  <Sym s="Q">Quantity sold</Sym>
</Symbols>

When a firm changes its price, two effects occur.

### Price Effect

A higher price means each unit sold generates more revenue.

### Quantity Effect

A higher price usually causes fewer units to be sold, reducing revenue.

The price elasticity of demand determines which effect is stronger.

| Demand                                | Price Increase          |
| ------------------------------------- | ----------------------- |
| Elastic ($\lvert E_d\rvert > 1$)      | Total revenue decreases |
| Inelastic ($\lvert E_d\rvert < 1$)    | Total revenue increases |
| Unit Elastic ($\lvert E_d\rvert = 1$) | Total revenue unchanged |

Total revenue is maximized when demand is unit elastic ($\lvert E_d\rvert = 1$). Otherwise price and revenue move relatively.

## Worked Examples

### Calculating PED (Midpoint Method)

The price of good X falls from \$40 to \$32, and quantity demanded rises from 200 to 350 units. Find the PED using the midpoint (arc elasticity) formula.

<Solution>

Midpoint formula:

$$
\%\Delta Q = \frac{350 - 200}{(350 + 200)/2} = \frac{150}{275} = 54.55\%
$$

$$
\%\Delta P = \frac{32 - 40}{(32 + 40)/2} = \frac{-8}{36} = -22.22\%
$$

$$
\lvert E_d \rvert = \left\lvert \frac{54.55\%}{-22.22\%} \right\rvert = 2.45
$$

Since $\lvert E_d \rvert > 1$, demand is elastic. A price cut raises total revenue.

</Solution>

### Quantity After a Price Cut

For ballpoint pens, $E_d = -3.0$. At Rs. 25, 300 pens are demanded. Find the quantity demanded at Rs. 20.

<Solution>

$$
\%\Delta P = \frac{20 - 25}{25} = -20\%
$$

$$
\%\Delta Q = E_d \times \%\Delta P = -3 \times -20\% = +60\%
$$

$$
Q = 300 \times 1.6 = 480
$$

</Solution>

### Price for a Target Quantity

For Milo, $E_d = -1.0$. At Rs. 60, 500 units are demanded. Find the price that gives a quantity demanded of 600.

<Solution>

$$
\%\Delta Q = \frac{600 - 500}{500} = +20\%
$$

$$
\%\Delta P = \frac{\%\Delta Q}{E_d} = \frac{20\%}{-1.0} = -20\%
$$

$$
P = 60 \times 0.8 = 48
$$

</Solution>
