Market equilibrium occurs at the point where the demand curve and
supply curve intersect.

The intersection determines:

- Equilibrium price  
  The price at which quantity demanded equals quantity supplied.
- Equilibrium quantity  
  The quantity demanded and supplied at the equilibrium price.

<MarketEquilibriumDiagram />

## Surplus

A surplus occurs when the market price is above the equilibrium price.

$$
Q_s > Q_d
$$

Producers want to sell more than consumers want to buy.

As a result, there is downward pressure on price, causing the price
to fall toward equilibrium.

## Shortage

A shortage occurs when the market price is below the equilibrium price.

$$
Q_d > Q_s
$$

Consumers want to buy more than producers want to sell.

As a result, there is upward pressure on price, causing the price
to rise toward equilibrium.

## Changes in Equilibrium

There are four basic types of changes.

| Change             | Curve Shift               | Equilibrium Price | Equilibrium Quantity |
| ------------------ | ------------------------- | ----------------: | -------------------: |
| Increase in Demand | Demand curve shifts right |                 ↑ |                    ↑ |
| Decrease in Demand | Demand curve shifts left  |                 ↓ |                    ↓ |
| Increase in Supply | Supply curve shifts right |                 ↓ |                    ↑ |
| Decrease in Supply | Supply curve shifts left  |                 ↑ |                    ↓ |

Market price can increase because of:

- Increase in demand
- Decrease in supply

Sometimes demand and supply change at the same time.

### Increase in Demand + Increase in Supply

Quantity must increase. Price depends on the relative change. May:

- Increase
- Decrease
- Remain unchanged

### Increase in Demand + Decrease in Supply

Price must increase. Quantity depends on the relative change. May:

- Increase
- Decrease
- Remain unchanged

## Consumer Surplus

Consumer surplus is the difference between the maximum price consumers are willing to pay and the price they actually pay.

On a linear demand curve it is the area of the triangle between the demand curve and the equilibrium price.

<ConsumerSurplusDiagram />

$$
CS = \frac{1}{2} \times Q^* \times (P_{\max} - P^*)
$$

Here:

<Symbols>
  <Sym s="Q^*">equilibrium quantity</Sym>
  <Sym s="P_{\max}">choke price, the price at which quantity demanded is 0</Sym>
  <Sym s="P^*">equilibrium price</Sym>
</Symbols>

## Producer Surplus

Producer surplus is the difference between the price producers receive and the minimum price they are willing to accept.

On a linear supply curve it is the area of the triangle between the equilibrium price and the supply curve.

<ProducerSurplusDiagram />

$$
PS = \frac{1}{2} \times Q^* \times (P^* - P_{\min})
$$

Here:

<Symbols>
  <Sym s="P_{\min}">the price at which quantity supplied is 0</Sym>
</Symbols>

## Worked Examples

### Consumer Surplus Calculation

Demand is $Q_d = 80 - 2P$ and supply is $Q_s = -20 + 3P$. Find the consumer surplus.

<Solution>

Set $Q_d = Q_s$.

$$
80 - 2P = -20 + 3P
$$

So $P^* = 20$ and $Q^* = 40$.

Inverse demand is $P = 40 - 0.5Q$, so $P_{\max} = 40$.

$$
CS = \frac{1}{2} \times 40 \times (40 - 20) = 400
$$

The consumer surplus is Rs. 400.

</Solution>

### Producer Surplus Calculation

Demand for bread is $Q_d = 110 - P$ and supply is $Q_s = -40 + 2P$. Find the producer surplus.

<Solution>

Set $Q_d = Q_s$.

$$
110 - P = -40 + 2P
$$

So $P^* = 50$ and $Q^* = 60$.

Inverse supply is $P = 20 + 0.5Q$, so $P_{\min} = 20$.

$$
PS = \frac{1}{2} \times 60 \times (50 - 20) = 900
$$

The producer surplus is 900.

</Solution>
