Refers to how output changes when the quantities of all inputs are changed.

## Types

### Constant Returns to Scale

<Aka>CRS</Aka>. Output increases in the same proportion as all inputs.

For example:

$$
\text{Inputs} \times 2 \rightarrow \text{Output} \times 2
$$

### Increasing Returns to Scale

<Aka>IRS</Aka>. Output increases by a greater proportion than the increase in
all inputs.

For example:

$$
\text{Inputs} \times 2 \rightarrow \text{Output} \times 3
$$

### Decreasing Returns to Scale

<Aka>DRS</Aka>. Output increases by a smaller proportion than the increase in
all inputs.

For example:

$$
\text{Inputs} \times 2 \rightarrow \text{Output} \times 1.5
$$

## Diminishing Marginal Returns vs Returns to Scale

### Diminishing Marginal Returns

- Short-run concept.
- At least one input is fixed.
- More of one variable input is added.
- Marginal product eventually decreases.

### Returns to Scale Characteristics

- Long-run concept.
- All inputs are variable.
- All inputs are changed together.
- Measures the response of output to proportional changes in all inputs.

## Economies and Diseconomies of Scale

Economies of scale are reductions in long-run average cost as output expands.

Sources:

- Optimum use of resources in the plant
- Inputs purchased in bulk
- Advertising cost spread over more units

Diseconomies of scale are increases in long-run average cost as output expands.

Sources:

- Coordination problems
- Weaker control
- Lower motivation in a very large firm

## Worked Examples

### Identifying a Diseconomy of Scale

Which of the following is a source of diseconomies of scale?

- Inefficiencies in the workforce
- Optimum use of resources in the plant
- Inputs purchased in bulk
- Reduced per-unit advertising cost

<Solution>

Inefficiencies in the workforce is the source of diseconomies of scale.

The other 3 are sources of economies of scale.

</Solution>
