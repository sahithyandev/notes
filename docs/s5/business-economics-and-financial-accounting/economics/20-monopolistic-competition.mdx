A market structure with many firms selling products that are similar but differentiated. Has limited [market power](./market-structure#market-power).

Example: many cafés sell coffee, but location, taste, design, and service let each café charge a slightly different price.

## Conditions

- Many firms  
  There are many firms competing for customers.
- Few artificial [barriers to entry](./market-structure#barriers-to-entry)  
  New firms can enter relatively easily.
- Slight control over price  
  Product differentiation gives firms some control over their prices.
- [Differentiated products](./product/#differentiated-products)

## Non-Price Competition

Firms attract customers without simply lowering their prices.

1. Characteristics of goods  
   Size, colour, shape, texture, or taste.
2. Location  
   A convenient location can attract customers.
3. Service level  
   Better service can allow a firm to charge a higher price.
4. Advertising and image  
   Advertising creates differences between competing products.

## Profit Maximization

A firm maximizes profit at the output where $MR = MC$. The price is read from the demand curve at that output.

For a linear inverse demand curve $P = a - bQ$:

$$
TR = aQ - bQ^2
$$

$$
MR = a - 2bQ
$$

In the long run, entry shifts each firm's demand curve left until economic profit is zero.

## Worked Examples

### Profit-Maximizing Price and Quantity

Demand is $Q = 100 - 2P$ and $MC = 10$. Find the profit-maximizing price and quantity.

<Solution>

Inverse demand:

$$
P = 50 - 0.5Q
$$

$$
TR = 50Q - 0.5Q^2, \quad MR = 50 - Q
$$

Set $MR = MC$.

$$
50 - Q = 10
$$

So $Q = 40$ and $P = 50 - 0.5(40) = 30$.

If $MC = AC$, the profit is

$$
(30 - 10) \times 40 = 800
$$

The profit-maximizing price is Rs. 30 and the quantity is 40.

</Solution>
