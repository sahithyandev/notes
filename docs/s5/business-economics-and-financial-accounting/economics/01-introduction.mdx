Economics is the study of how people satisfy their needs and wants, and how society manages its scarce resources.

Example: spending $20 on transport leaves less income for food or entertainment. Economics studies that choice and its opportunity cost.

Economists study how people make decisions: how much they work, what they buy, how much they save, how they invest.

### Needs

Basic necessities a person must have to survive.

For example: food, water, warmth, shelter, clothing.

### Wants

Desires people have for things beyond survival.

For example: bigger homes, phones, and similar.

## Statement

### Positive Statement

A descriptive claim about how the world is. Can be tested.

For example: higher interest rates encourage people to save more.

### Normative Statement

A prescriptive claim about how the world ought to be. Involves values, ethics, and political philosophy, not just facts.

For example: the government should tax the rich to help the poor.

## Types

### Microeconomics

Study of how households and firms make decisions and interact in specific markets. Deals with individual prices, consumption, and production. Simpler in scope, easier to isolate variables.

### Macroeconomics

Study of economy-wide phenomena. Deals with national income, total output, total consumption, and the general price level. More complex due to interdependencies across sectors.

Macroeconomic policy aims to improve national economic performance through 6 major objectives:

- Economic growth  
  Increase real output and real income over time.
- Full employment  
  Provide jobs for people willing and able to work.
- Price stability  
  Keep inflation low and predictable.
- Balance of payments equilibrium  
  Maintain sustainable external payments and receipts.
- Equitable income distribution  
  Reduce extreme inequality through taxes, transfers, and public services.
- Economic stability  
  Reduce severe fluctuations in output, employment, inflation, interest rates, and the exchange rate.

The objectives can conflict. Expansionary policy can increase growth and employment while also increasing inflation. Higher interest rates can reduce inflation while slowing investment and employment.

## Importance for Engineering

Engineering decisions allocate scarce resources among competing uses. Economics provides criteria for selecting technically feasible and financially viable alternatives.

Economics helps engineers:

- Compare capital cost, operating cost, expected return, and risk.
- Evaluate opportunity cost when equipment, labour, or time has competing uses.
- Choose production levels using marginal cost and marginal revenue.
- Estimate demand before designing or scaling a product.
- Evaluate the effects of taxes, subsidies, interest rates, inflation, and exchange rates.
- Compare automation with additional labour.
- Include environmental externalities and life-cycle costs.

For example, a mechanical engineer choosing between 2 manufacturing machines must compare purchase price, energy use, maintenance, capacity, expected life, and opportunity cost. The technically superior machine is not necessarily the economically superior choice.

## Three Key Issues in Economics

- What should be produced, and how scarce resources are allocated between the possible kinds of production.
- How it should be produced, and which production procedure to use, weighed against its efficiency.
- For whom it should be produced, and how output is distributed among the final customers.

## Worked Examples

### Identifying a Normative Statement

Which of the following is a normative statement?

- People work harder if the wage is higher
- The unemployment rate should be lower
- Large government deficits cause an economy to grow more slowly
- Printing too much money causes inflation

<Solution>

"The unemployment rate should be lower" is normative. It is a value judgment about what ought to be and cannot be tested with data.

The other 3 are positive statements and can be tested.

</Solution>

### Sri Lankan Economic and Debt Crisis

What caused the recent Sri Lankan economic and debt crisis, and what were its effects on engineering firms?

<Solution>

The recent crisis resulted from long-term structural weaknesses combined with immediate external and policy shocks.

Major causes included:

- Persistent fiscal deficits and weak government revenue.
- Large external debt repayments and limited foreign-exchange reserves.
- Tax reductions that weakened government revenue before the crisis.
- The COVID-19 shock to tourism, exports, and remittances.
- Foreign-exchange shortages that restricted imports and debt servicing.
- Policy errors that reduced agricultural output and increased food imports.
- Loss of access to international capital markets.
- Global increases in fuel, food, and other import prices.

Major effects included:

- Severe shortages of fuel, medicine, food, and industrial inputs.
- Long power cuts and reduced industrial production.
- Rapid inflation and a sharp fall in real household income.
- Currency depreciation and higher costs for imported machinery and components.
- Business closures, unemployment, and reduced investment.
- Increased poverty, malnutrition, and migration.
- Sovereign default and the need for international financial assistance.

For an engineering firm, the crisis increased the rupee cost of imported equipment, delayed spare-parts deliveries, reduced production reliability, and made project cost estimation more uncertain.

</Solution>
