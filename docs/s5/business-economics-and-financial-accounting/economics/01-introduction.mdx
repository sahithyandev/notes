Economics is the study of how people satisfy their needs and wants, and how society manages its scarce resources.

Economists study how people make decisions: how much they work, what they buy, how much they save, how they invest.

## Needs and wants

- Needs  
  Basic necessities a person must have to survive: food, water, warmth, shelter, clothing.
- Wants  
  Desires people have for things beyond survival: bigger homes, phones, and similar.

## Positive vs normative statements

- Positive statement  
  A descriptive claim about how the world is. Can be tested. Example: higher interest rates encourage people to save more.
- Normative statement  
  A prescriptive claim about how the world ought to be. Involves values, ethics, and political philosophy, not just facts. Example: the government should tax the rich to help the poor.

## Microeconomics vs macroeconomics

- Microeconomics  
  Study of how households and firms make decisions and interact in specific markets. Deals with individual prices, consumption, and production. Simpler in scope, easier to isolate variables.
- Macroeconomics  
  Study of economy-wide phenomena. Deals with national income, total output, total consumption, and the general price level. More complex due to interdependencies across sectors.

## Three key issues in economics

- What should be produced, and how scarce resources are allocated between the possible kinds of production.
- How it should be produced: which production procedure to use, and its efficiency.
- For whom it should be produced: who the final customers are and how output is distributed to them.

## Basic concepts

- Scarcity  
  Society has limited resources and cannot produce all the goods and services people wish to have. A universal problem, not limited to poor nations. All products that have a price are relatively scarce.
- Choice  
  Because resources are limited but wants are unlimited, society must choose how to allocate scarce resources between alternatives.
- Opportunity cost  
  The next best option given up when making a choice. Measured in alternatives given up rather than in money.
- Trade-off  
  Giving up some of one thing to get more of another. If a drink costs Rs. 250 and a cinema ticket Rs. 750, the cost of one cinema visit is three drinks.
- Rational people  
  A rational decision maker compares marginal benefit and marginal cost, and acts only if the marginal benefit exceeds the marginal cost.
- Marginal change  
  A small incremental adjustment to a plan of action.

## Factors of production

- Land  
  All natural resources used in production, renewable and non-renewable. Return: rent.
- Labour  
  Physical and mental effort of human beings used in production. Return: wages.
- Capital  
  Man-made aids to further production. Return: interest.
- Entrepreneurship  
  Managing, organizing, and planning the other three factors, and bearing risk. Return: profit.

## Economic goods vs free goods

- Economic good  
  Uses scarce resources in its production, so its marginal cost is greater than zero. Example: a paper clip.
- Free good  
  Does not require scarce resources, so its marginal cost is zero. Example: air, sunlight.

## Types of products in an economy

- Consumer goods  
  Purchased and consumed directly by individuals and households. Durable (appliances, cars) or non-durable (food, clothing).
- Capital goods  
  Also called producer or intermediate goods. Used by businesses to produce other goods and services: machinery, equipment, factories, infrastructure.
- Services  
  Intangible actions provided to meet a need, leaving no physical product. Consumed at the time of production: healthcare, education, transport, consulting.

## Economic models

### Circular flow diagram

Shows how the economy is organized and how firms and households interact with each other.

### Production possibilities frontier

The various combinations of two outputs an economy can produce given its fixed factors of production and technology. Demonstrates scarcity, choice, and opportunity cost.

Assumptions:

- The economy produces only 2 products.
- Given resources remain constant.
- Technology and other factors remain constant during the period.
- Resources are fully utilized with maximum efficiency.
- Resources can transfer from one product to the other.

Positions:

- On the boundary  
  Resources fully and efficiently employed.
- Inside the boundary  
  Resources unemployed or inefficiently employed.
- Outside the boundary  
  Not attainable with current resources.

The curve has a negative slope: more of one good requires producing less of the other.

Shapes:

- Concave  
  Increasing opportunity cost. Resources are non-homogeneous and not equally efficient across industries.
- Straight line  
  Constant opportunity cost. Homogeneous resources, equally efficient in both industries.
- Convex  
  Decreasing opportunity cost.

Shifts and pivots:

- More available resources or economic growth shift the curve outwards.
- Technological improvement in one product pivots the curve from the relevant axis; improvement in both shifts it outwards.
- Production can increase without opportunity cost by using unemployed resources or raising resource efficiency.

## Economic systems

- Traditional system  
  Behaviour based on tradition, customs, and habits. Property held in common. What, how, and for whom to produce are determined by what happened in the past. Works better in unchanging environments. Example: communities in the Canadian Arctic.
- Command system  
  Also called a centrally planned economy. A central authority makes most economic decisions and sets prices. The state owns major industries. Limited consumer choice and minimal competition. Example: North Korea.
- Pure market system  
  Also called free market or capitalism. Individual decisions of consumers and firms collectively allocate resources through unhindered markets. The state only provides legal structure and external defense. Production is driven by consumer demand and profit; goods go to those who can afford them.
- Mixed system  
  Combines elements of all three. Both markets and government affect resource allocation and income distribution. Government enforces ownership and contract law, maintains monetary stability, corrects market failures, provides merit goods such as free health and education, and redistributes income. In practice every economy is mixed.

Market failure is a situation where the free market does not work well, such as over-exploited natural resources, under-provided public goods, or externalities imposed on society by the private sector.
