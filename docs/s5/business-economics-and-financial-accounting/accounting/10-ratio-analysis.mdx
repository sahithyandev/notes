Ratio analysis computes and interprets the relationship between 2 related or interdependent components of financial statements over an accounting period, to evaluate a company's operations, profitability, liquidity, solvency, and efficiency.

A ratio on its own is just a signal. The value should be compared with the same company's past, a competitor or an industry benchmark.

<Note>The equations for the ratios will be given the exam paper.</Note>

## Liquidity Ratios

Assess a company's ability to meet short-term liabilities using current assets or cash reserves.

Always current liabilities are used in the denominator of the ratio.

### Current Ratio

<Formula>

$$
\text{Current Ratio} = \frac{\text{Current Assets}}{\text{Current Liabilities}}
$$

</Formula>

Higher ratio indicates a better short-term liquidity position. Excessively high ratio may indicate inefficient use of assets.

### Quick Ratio

<Aka>Acid-Test Ratio</Aka>. Modified version of Current Ratio. Uses quick assets
instead of current assets. Gives a more conservative liquidity measure.

<Formula>

$$
\text{Quick Assets} = \text{Current Assets} - \text{Inventory} - \text{Prepaid Expenses}
$$

</Formula>

Using these quick assets, the Quick Ratio is calculated as follows:

<Formula>

$$
\text{Quick Ratio} = \frac{\text{Quick Assets}}{\text{Current Liabilities}}
$$

</Formula>

Higher ratio suggests better short-term liquidity without relying on inventory sale.

### Cash Ratio

The most conservative liquidity ratio, using only cash and cash equivalents against current liabilities.

<Formula>

$$
\text{Cash Ratio} = \frac{\text{Cash and Cash Equivalents}}{\text{Current Liabilities}}
$$

</Formula>

Higher ratio indicates stronger ability to meet short-term liabilities using cash reserves.

## Financial Leverage Ratios

<Aka>Solvency Ratios</Aka>. Assess a company's ability to meet long-term debt
obligations and overall financial stability.

Total Debt is the sum of interest-bearing borrowings: short-term loans, long-term loans, debentures / bonds payable, and lease liabilities.

### Debt-to-Equity Ratio

Measures the proportion of total debt to shareholders' equity.

<Formula>

$$
\text{Debt-to-Equity Ratio} = \frac{\text{Total Debt}}{\text{Total Equity}}
$$

</Formula>

Higher ratio indicates greater financial leverage and risk.

### Debt Ratio

Measures the proportion of total assets financed by debt.

<Formula>

$$
\text{Debt Ratio} = \frac{\text{Total Debt}}{\text{Total Assets}}
$$

</Formula>

Higher ratio suggests greater reliance on debt financing and risk of insolvency.

### Interest Coverage Ratio

Evaluates a company's ability to meet interest payments on outstanding debt.

<Formula>

$$
\text{Interest Coverage Ratio} = \frac{\text{EBIT}}{\text{Interest Expense}}
$$

</Formula>

EBIT stands for Earnings Before Interest and Taxes which means operating profit before deducting interest expense and income tax.

Higher ratio indicates a lower risk of default.

## Efficiency Ratios

<Aka>Activity or Asset Management Ratios</Aka>. Measure how effectively a
company manages its assets, liabilities, and resources.

### Inventory Turnover Ratio

Measures how quickly a company sells and replaces its inventory.

<Formula>

$$
\text{Inventory Turnover Ratio} = \frac{\text{Cost of Goods Sold}}{\text{Average Inventory}}
$$

</Formula>

Cost of Goods Sold (COGS) is the direct cost of producing or acquiring goods sold in the period.

Average Inventory is the average of the beginning and ending inventory levels.

Higher ratio suggests efficient inventory management and minimized carrying costs. Extremely high ratio may indicate stockouts.

### Days Sales of Inventory

Number of days it takes to sell inventory.

<Formula>

$$
\text{Days Sales of Inventory} = \frac{365}{\text{Inventory Turnover Ratio}}
$$

</Formula>

Lower value suggests faster inventory turnover.

### Accounts Receivable Turnover Ratio

Evaluates how efficiently a company collects receivables from customers.

<Formula>

$$
\text{Accounts Receivable Turnover Ratio} = \frac{\text{Net Credit Sales}}{\text{Average Accounts Receivable}}
$$

</Formula>

Higher value suggests faster collection from customers.

### Days Sales Outstanding

Average number of days to collect payment from customers.

<Formula>

$$
\text{Days Sales Outstanding} = \frac{365}{\text{Accounts Receivable Turnover Ratio}}
$$

</Formula>

Lower value suggests faster cash conversion.

### Asset Turnover Ratio

Measures how efficiently a company utilizes assets to generate sales.

<Formula>

$$
\text{Asset Turnover Ratio} = \frac{\text{Net Sales}}{\text{Average Total Assets}}
$$

</Formula>

Higher ratio indicates better operational efficiency and productivity.

### Payable Turnover Ratio

Assesses how efficiently a company manages trade payables.

<Formula>

$$
\text{Payable Turnover Ratio} = \frac{\text{Net Credit Purchases}}{\text{Average Accounts Payable}}
$$

</Formula>

Higher ratio suggests prompt supplier payment and effective cash management.

### Days Payable Outstanding

Average number of days to pay suppliers, aka. creditor days.

<Formula>

$$
\text{Days Payable Outstanding} = \frac{365}{\text{Payable Turnover Ratio}}
$$

</Formula>

Higher value preserves cash but may strain supplier relationships. Lower value indicates prompter supplier payment.

### Cash Conversion Cycle

Time taken to convert investments in inventory and other resources into cash from sales.

<Formula>

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{Cash Conversion Cycle} &= \text{Days Sales of Inventory} \\
&+ \text{Days Sales Outstanding} \\
&- \text{Days Payable Outstanding}
\end{aligned}
$$

</Formula>

Lower cycle indicates faster conversion and more efficient working capital management.

## Profitability Ratios

Measure a company's ability to generate profit relative to sales, assets, equity, and other factors.

### Gross Profit Margin

Percentage of sales revenue remaining after deducting cost of goods sold.

<Formula>

$$
\text{Gross Profit Margin} = \frac{\text{Gross Profit}}{\text{Revenue}} \times 100
$$

</Formula>

Higher margin indicates more profit from core operations.

### Operating Profit Margin

Percentage of operating income relative to revenue.

<Formula>

$$
\text{Operating Profit Margin} = \frac{\text{Operating Income}}{\text{Revenue}} \times 100
$$

</Formula>

Higher margin indicates more profit from core business activities, excluding non-operating expenses.

### Net Profit Margin

Percentage of net income relative to revenue.

<Formula>

$$
\text{Net Profit Margin} = \frac{\text{Net Income}}{\text{Revenue}} \times 100
$$

</Formula>

Higher margin indicates more profit after all expenses, including taxes and interest.

### Return on Assets

Percentage of EBIT relative to average total assets.

<Formula>

$$
\text{Return on Assets} = \frac{\text{EBIT}}{\text{Average Total Assets}} \times 100
$$

</Formula>

Higher value indicates more profit per dollar of assets employed.

### Return on Equity

Percentage of net income relative to average shareholders' equity.

<Formula>

$$
\text{Return on Equity} = \frac{\text{Net Income}}{\text{Average Shareholders' Equity}} \times 100
$$

</Formula>

Higher value indicates more profit per dollar of shareholders' equity invested.

### Return on Capital Employed

Percentage of operating profit relative to capital employed.

<Formula>

$$
\text{Return on Capital Employed} = \frac{\text{EBIT}}{\text{Capital Employed}} \times 100
$$

</Formula>

Capital Employed:

<Formula>

$$
\text{Capital Employed} = \text{Total Assets} - \text{Current Liabilities}
$$

</Formula>

Equivalently, shareholders' equity plus non-current liabilities.

Higher value indicates more profit per dollar of long-term capital invested.

## Market Value Ratios

<Aka>Investor/Market Value Ratios</Aka>. Assess the relationship between a
company's stock price and its earnings, book value, or other financial metrics.

### Earnings per Share

Portion of a company's profit allocated to each outstanding share of common stock.

<Formula>

$$
\text{Earnings per Share} = \frac{\text{Net Income} - \text{Preferred Dividends}}{\text{Weighted Average Shares Outstanding}}
$$

</Formula>

Higher EPS may indicate higher earnings potential and attractiveness to investors.

### Price-to-Earnings Ratio

Compares a company's stock price to its earnings per share.

<Formula>

$$
\text{Price-to-Earnings Ratio} = \frac{\text{Market Price per Share}}{\text{Earnings per Share}}
$$

</Formula>

Higher ratio suggests higher investor confidence and expectations of future growth.

### Price-to-Book Ratio

Compares a company's stock price to its book value per share.

<Formula>

$$
\text{Price-to-Book Ratio} = \frac{\text{Market Price per Share}}{\text{Book Value per Share}}
$$

</Formula>

Higher ratio suggests investors are willing to pay a premium for the company's book value.

### Dividend Yield

Annual dividend income generated by a stock relative to its market price.

<Formula>

$$
\text{Dividend Yield} = \frac{\text{Annual Dividend per Share}}{\text{Market Price per Share}} \times 100
$$

</Formula>

Higher yield may be attractive to income-seeking investors.

Current liabilities include a short-term loan of $30000$, accounts payable of $70000$ and accrued expenses of $20000$. Total debt is the short-term loan plus long-term loans.

<Solution>

The derived figures used below are:

$$
\begin{array}{llll}
\text{Total Assets} & = & 210000 + 390000 & = 600000 \\
\text{Total Debt} & = & 30000 + 200000 & = 230000 \\
\text{Quick Assets} & = & 210000 - 100000 - 10000 & = 100000 \\
\text{Gross Profit} & = & 800000 - 480000 & = 320000 \\
\text{EBIT} & = & 320000 - 180000 & = 140000 \\
\text{Net Income} & = & 140000 - 14000 - 31500 & = 94500
\end{array}
$$

Average balances for the efficiency and return ratios:

$$
\begin{array}{llll}
\text{Average Inventory} & = & \dfrac{80000 + 100000}{2} & = 90000 \\
\text{Average Accounts Receivable} & = & \dfrac{60000 + 80000}{2} & = 70000 \\
\text{Average Accounts Payable} & = & \dfrac{50000 + 70000}{2} & = 60000 \\
\text{Average Total Assets} & = & \dfrac{560000 + 600000}{2} & = 580000 \\
\text{Average Shareholders' Equity} & = & \dfrac{250000 + 280000}{2} & = 265000
\end{array}
$$

Liquidity ratios:

$$
\begin{array}{llll}
\text{Current Ratio} & = & \dfrac{210000}{120000} & = 1.75 \\
\text{Quick Ratio} & = & \dfrac{100000}{120000} & = 0.83 \\
\text{Cash Ratio} & = & \dfrac{20000}{120000} & = 0.17
\end{array}
$$

Leverage ratios:

$$
\begin{array}{llll}
\text{Debt-to-Equity Ratio} & = & \dfrac{230000}{280000} & = 0.82 \\
\text{Debt Ratio} & = & \dfrac{230000}{600000} & = 0.38 \\
\text{Interest Coverage Ratio} & = & \dfrac{140000}{14000} & = 10
\end{array}
$$

Efficiency ratios:

$$
\begin{array}{llll}
\text{Inventory Turnover Ratio} & = & \dfrac{480000}{90000} & = 5.33 \\
\text{Days Sales of Inventory} & = & \dfrac{365}{5.33} & = 68.5 \\
\text{Accounts Receivable Turnover Ratio} & = & \dfrac{800000}{70000} & = 11.43 \\
\text{Days Sales Outstanding} & = & \dfrac{365}{11.43} & = 31.9 \\
\text{Asset Turnover Ratio} & = & \dfrac{800000}{580000} & = 1.38 \\
\text{Payable Turnover Ratio} & = & \dfrac{500000}{60000} & = 8.33 \\
\text{Days Payable Outstanding} & = & \dfrac{365}{8.33} & = 43.8 \\
\text{Cash Conversion Cycle} & = & 68.5 + 31.9 - 43.8 & = 56.6 \text{ days}
\end{array}
$$

Profitability ratios:

$$
\begin{array}{llll}
\text{Gross Profit Margin} & = & \dfrac{320000}{800000} \times 100 & = 40\% \\
\text{Operating Profit Margin} & = & \dfrac{140000}{800000} \times 100 & = 17.5\% \\
\text{Net Profit Margin} & = & \dfrac{94500}{800000} \times 100 & = 11.8\% \\
\text{Return on Assets} & = & \dfrac{140000}{580000} \times 100 & = 24.1\% \\
\text{Return on Equity} & = & \dfrac{94500}{265000} \times 100 & = 35.7\% \\
\text{Return on Capital Employed} & = & \dfrac{140000}{600000 - 120000} \times 100 & = 29.2\%
\end{array}
$$

Market value ratios:

$$
\begin{array}{llll}
\text{Earnings per Share} & = & \dfrac{94500}{100000} & = 0.95 \\
\text{Price-to-Earnings Ratio} & = & \dfrac{12}{0.95} & = 12.6
\end{array}
$$

</Solution>
