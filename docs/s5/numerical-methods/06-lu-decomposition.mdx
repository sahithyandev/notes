The process of factorizing a square matrix into 2 [triangular matrices](/s1/mathematics/matrices/introduction#triangular-matrix): a lower triangular matrix ($L$) and an upper triangular matrix ($U$).
Introduced by Alan Turing in 1948.

$$
A = LU
$$

## Finding $L$ and $U$ Without Pivoting

### Using Gaussian Elimination

Can be used when [Gaussian elimination](/s5/numerical-methods/gaussian-elimination) can be performed on $A\vec{x} = \vec{b}$ without row interchanges, $A$ factors as $A = LU$, where

$$
m_{ji} = \frac{a_{ji}^{(i)}}{a_{ii}^{(i)}}
$$

is the multiplier used to eliminate entry $(j, i)$ at step $i$, $U$ is the row-echelon form produced by elimination, and $L$ collects the multipliers:

$$
U = \begin{pmatrix}
a_{11}^{(1)} & a_{12}^{(1)} & \cdots & a_{1n}^{(1)} \\
0 & a_{22}^{(2)} & \cdots & a_{2n}^{(2)} \\
\vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\
0 & \cdots & \cdots & a_{nn}^{(n)} \end{pmatrix}

\; \text{and} \;

L = \begin{pmatrix}
1 & 0 & \cdots & 0 \\
m_{21} & 1 & \cdots & \vdots \\
\vdots & \vdots & \ddots & 0 \\
m_{n1} & \cdots & m_{n,n-1} & 1 \end{pmatrix}
$$

$L$ and $U$ are produced together in 1 pass of elimination.

- For $i = 1, \ldots, n-1$, use row $i$ as the pivot row.
- For each row $j > i$, compute $m_{ji} = a_{ji}^{(i)} / a_{ii}^{(i)}$.
- Eliminate $a_{ji}$ with $\text{row}_j \leftarrow \text{row}_j - m_{ji} \cdot \text{row}_i$.
- Place $m_{ji}$ at position $(j, i)$ of $L$.

$U$ is the result of elimination. $L$ is the record of multipliers used to produce it.

### Direct Computation Method

$L$ and $U$ can also be computed directly by equating entries of $A$ with entries of the product $LU$.

Entry $(i, j)$ of $LU$ is row $i$ of $L$ multiplied by column $j$ of $U$.

$$
a_{ij} = \sum_{k=1}^{n} l_{ik} u_{kj}
$$

$L$ is lower triangular and $U$ is upper triangular.

- $l_{ik} = 0$ for $k > i$.
- $u_{kj} = 0$ for $k > j$.

<LuZeroPattern />

So the sum stops at $k = \min(i, j)$.

Setting $l_{ii} = 1$ (Doolittle's method), for $i = 1, \ldots, n$ do 2 steps.

- Row $i$ of $U$, for $j = i, \ldots, n$

$$
u_{ij} = a_{ij} - \sum_{k=1}^{i-1} l_{ik} u_{kj}
$$

- Column $i$ of $L$, for $j = i+1, \ldots, n$

$$
l_{ji} = \frac{1}{u_{ii}} \left[ a_{ji} - \sum_{k=1}^{i-1} l_{jk} u_{ki} \right]
$$

Each formula is the entry of $A$ minus the terms that use already known entries of $L$ and $U$. For $i = 1$ the sums are empty, so row 1 of $U$ is row 1 of $A$.

<Note>

Crout's method sets $u_{ii} = 1$ instead. The division then moves from $l_{ji}$ to $u_{ij}$.

</Note>

## Pivoting

LU decomposition can fail when the top-left entry of $A$ is $0$ or small compared to other entries.

Pivoting rearranges the rows and/or columns of $A$ to place a larger element in the top-left position, mitigating this. Multiple pivoting algorithms exist.

## Applications

- Solving $A\vec{x} = \vec{b}$, used for finding current in a circuit and solving discrete dynamical systems.
- Finding the inverse of a matrix.
- Finding the determinant of a matrix.

Applicable whenever a problem can be modeled in matrix form, since triangular matrices are easy to compute with.

### Solving $A\vec{x} = \vec{b}$

Suppose $A = LU$ and $A\vec{x} = LU\vec{x} = \vec{b}$ must be solved.

- Set $\vec{w} = U\vec{x}$.
- Solve $L\vec{w} = \vec{b}$ for $\vec{w}$ by forward substitution, since $L$ is lower triangular.
- Solve $U\vec{x} = \vec{w}$ for $\vec{x}$ by back substitution, since $U$ is upper triangular. This $\vec{x}$ solves the original system.

This uses $L$ to perform row operations on $U$ to solve the system.

The factors depend only on $A$, so they are reused for every new right-hand side. Nodal analysis of a linear circuit gives $Y\vec{v} = \vec{i}$, where $Y$ is the admittance matrix. Changing the sources changes $\vec{i}$ alone. $Y$ is factored once. Each new $\vec{i}$ costs only the 2 substitutions.

Factorization costs $O(n^3)$, since each of the $n-1$ elimination steps touches the remaining submatrix. Forward and back substitution are each $O(n^2)$. The factorization dominates and is performed once, so solving $k$ systems that share $A$ costs $O(n^3) + O(k n^2)$ in total. Gaussian elimination on each system separately repeats the $O(n^3)$ reduction $k$ times.

Space is $O(n^2)$, since $L$ and $U$ together occupy as much storage as $A$.

### Finding the Inverse

Let $X$ be the inverse of $A$, with columns $\vec{x}_1, \ldots, \vec{x}_n$. The equation $AX = I$ splits into $n$ systems that share the same $A$.

$$
A\vec{x}_j = \vec{e}_j, \quad j = 1, \ldots, n
$$

Solve each system with the existing factors. The solution $\vec{x}_j$ is column $j$ of $X$, so the columns collected in order form $A^{-1}$.

The inverse is the previous case with $n$ right-hand sides, so a single factorization and $n$ pairs of substitutions give $A^{-1}$ in $O(n^3)$ time. Gaussian elimination on each column separately costs $O(n^4)$ in total.

<Note>

Computing $A^{-1}$ outright is rarely needed. Solving $A\vec{x} = \vec{b}$ directly is cheaper and avoids the rounding error introduced by forming the inverse.

</Note>

### Finding the Determinant

For $A = LU$,

$$
\det A = \det L \det U
$$

$L$ from Doolittle's method is unit lower triangular, so $\det L = 1$. $U$ is triangular, so $\det U$ is the product of its diagonal entries.

$$
\det A = \prod_{i=1}^{n} u_{ii}
$$

A row interchange during pivoting flips the sign of the determinant. With $s$ interchanges,

$$
\det A = (-1)^{s} \prod_{i=1}^{n} u_{ii}
$$

The determinant is read off the factorization already computed for solving systems. It adds no cost beyond the factorization itself.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1

For

$$
A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 & -1 \\ -3 & -1 & 2 \\ -2 & 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}
$$

find $L$ and $U$.

<Solution>

<Method label="Gaussian Elimination">

$$
m_{21} = \frac{-3}{2} = -\frac{3}{2}, \quad m_{31} = \frac{-2}{2} = -1
$$

$$
\xrightarrow[R_3 + R_1]{R_2 + \frac{3}{2}R_1}
\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 & -1 \\ 0 & \frac{1}{2} & \frac{1}{2} \\ 0 & 2 & 1 \end{pmatrix}
$$

$$
m_{32} = \frac{2}{\frac{1}{2}} = 4
$$

$$
\xrightarrow{R_3 - 4R_2}
\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 & -1 \\ 0 & \frac{1}{2} & \frac{1}{2} \\ 0 & 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix}
$$

Collecting the multipliers

$$
L = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ -\frac{3}{2} & 1 & 0 \\ -1 & 4 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \quad
U = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 & -1 \\ 0 & \frac{1}{2} & \frac{1}{2} \\ 0 & 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix}
$$

</Method>

<Method label="Doolittle's Method">

Row 1 of $U$: $u_{11} = 2$, $u_{12} = 1$, $u_{13} = -1$

Column 1 of $L$: $l_{21} = -\frac{3}{2}$, $l_{31} = -1$

Row 2 of $U$: $u_{22} = \frac{1}{2}$, $u_{23} = \frac{1}{2}$

Column 2 of $L$: $l_{32} = 4$

Row 3 of $U$: $u_{33} = -1$

### Example 2

Using the $L$ and $U$ from Example 1, solve $A\vec{x} = \vec{b}$ for

$$
\vec{b} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}
$$

<Solution>

<Method label="Forward and Back Substitution">

From Example 1,

$$
L = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ -\frac{3}{2} & 1 & 0 \\ -1 & 4 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \quad
U = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 & -1 \\ 0 & \frac{1}{2} & \frac{1}{2} \\ 0 & 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix}
$$

Solve $L\vec{w} = \vec{b}$ by forward substitution.

$$
w_1 = 1
$$

$$
-\frac{3}{2} w_1 + w_2 = 0 \implies w_2 = \frac{3}{2}
$$

$$
-w_1 + 4w_2 + w_3 = 1 \implies w_3 = -4
$$

$$
\vec{w} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ \frac{3}{2} \\ -4 \end{pmatrix}
$$

Solve $U\vec{x} = \vec{w}$ by back substitution.

$$
-x_3 = -4 \implies x_3 = 4
$$

$$
\frac{1}{2} x_2 + \frac{1}{2}(4) = \frac{3}{2} \implies x_2 = -1
$$

$$
2x_1 + x_2 - x_3 = 1 \implies x_1 = 3
$$

$$
\vec{x} = \begin{pmatrix} 3 \\ -1 \\ 4 \end{pmatrix}
$$

</Method>

</Solution>

### Example 3

Find the determinant of

$$
A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 1 \\ 3 & 8 & 5 \\ 2 & 7 & 9 \end{pmatrix}
$$

using LU decomposition.

<Solution>

<Method label="Gaussian Elimination">

Elimination on $A$

$$
m_{21} = 3, \quad m_{31} = 2
$$

$$
\xrightarrow[R_3 - 2R_1]{R_2 - 3R_1}
\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 2 & 2 \\ 0 & 3 & 7 \end{pmatrix}
$$

$$
m_{32} = \frac{3}{2}
$$

$$
\xrightarrow{R_3 - \frac{3}{2}R_2}
\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 2 & 2 \\ 0 & 0 & 4 \end{pmatrix}
$$

$$
L = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 3 & 1 & 0 \\ 2 & \frac{3}{2} & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \quad
U = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 2 & 2 \\ 0 & 0 & 4 \end{pmatrix}
$$

The determinant is the product of the diagonal entries of $U$ ($L$ has unit diagonal).

$$
\det A = 1 \cdot 2 \cdot 4 = 8
$$

</Method>

<Method label="Doolittle's Method">

Row 1 of $U$: $u_{11} = 1$, $u_{12} = 2$, $u_{13} = 1$

Column 1 of $L$: $l_{21} = 3$, $l_{31} = 2$

Row 2 of $U$: $u_{22} = 2$, $u_{23} = 2$

Column 2 of $L$: $l_{32} = \frac{3}{2}$

Row 3 of $U$: $u_{33} = 4$

$$

$$
\det A = 1 \cdot 2 \cdot 4 = 8

</Method>

Column 1 of $L$: $l_{11} = 1$, $l_{21} = 3$, $l_{31} = 2$

Row 1 of $U$: $u_{12} = 2$, $u_{13} = 1$

Column 2 of $L$: $l_{22} = 2$, $l_{32} = 3$

The determinant is the product of the diagonal entries of $L$ ($U$ has unit diagonal).

### Example 4

Using the $L$ and $U$ from Example 1, find the inverse of

$$
A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 & -1 \\ -3 & -1 & 2 \\ -2 & 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}
$$

<Solution>

<Method label="Forward and Back Substitution">

Each column $\vec{x}_j$ of $A^{-1}$ solves $LU\vec{x}_j = \vec{e}_j$. From Example 1,

$$
L = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ -\frac{3}{2} & 1 & 0 \\ -1 & 4 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \quad
U = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 & -1 \\ 0 & \frac{1}{2} & \frac{1}{2} \\ 0 & 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix}
$$

Column 1, $\vec{e}_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}$

Solve $L\vec{w}_1 = \vec{e}_1$ by forward substitution.

$$
w_1 = 1
$$

$$
-\frac{3}{2} w_1 + w_2 = 0 \implies w_2 = \frac{3}{2}
$$

$$
-w_1 + 4w_2 + w_3 = 0 \implies w_3 = -5
$$

$$
\vec{w}_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ \frac{3}{2} \\ -5 \end{pmatrix}
$$

Solve $U\vec{x}_1 = \vec{w}_1$ by back substitution.

$$
-x_3 = -5 \implies x_3 = 5
$$

$$
\frac{1}{2} x_2 + \frac{1}{2}(5) = \frac{3}{2} \implies x_2 = -2
$$

$$
2x_1 + x_2 - x_3 = 1 \implies x_1 = 4
$$

$$
\vec{x}_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 4 \\ -2 \\ 5 \end{pmatrix}
$$

Column 2, $\vec{e}_2 = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}$

Solve $L\vec{w}_2 = \vec{e}_2$ by forward substitution.

$$
w_1 = 0
$$

$$
-\frac{3}{2} w_1 + w_2 = 1 \implies w_2 = 1
$$

$$
-w_1 + 4w_2 + w_3 = 0 \implies w_3 = -4
$$

$$
\vec{w}_2 = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ -4 \end{pmatrix}
$$

Solve $U\vec{x}_2 = \vec{w}_2$ by back substitution.

$$
-x_3 = -4 \implies x_3 = 4
$$

$$
\frac{1}{2} x_2 + \frac{1}{2}(4) = 1 \implies x_2 = -2
$$

$$
2x_1 + x_2 - x_3 = 0 \implies x_1 = 3
$$

$$
\vec{x}_2 = \begin{pmatrix} 3 \\ -2 \\ 4 \end{pmatrix}
$$

Column 3, $\vec{e}_3 = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}$

Solve $L\vec{w}_3 = \vec{e}_3$ by forward substitution.

$$
w_1 = 0
$$

$$
-\frac{3}{2} w_1 + w_2 = 0 \implies w_2 = 0
$$

$$
-w_1 + 4w_2 + w_3 = 1 \implies w_3 = 1
$$

$$
\vec{w}_3 = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}
$$

Solve $U\vec{x}_3 = \vec{w}_3$ by back substitution.

$$
-x_3 = 1 \implies x_3 = -1
$$

$$
\frac{1}{2} x_2 + \frac{1}{2}(-1) = 0 \implies x_2 = 1
$$

$$
2x_1 + x_2 - x_3 = 0 \implies x_1 = -1
$$

$$
\vec{x}_3 = \begin{pmatrix} -1 \\ 1 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix}
$$

Assembling the columns,

$$
A^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 3 & -1 \\ -2 & -2 & 1 \\ 5 & 4 & -1 \end{pmatrix}
$$

</Method>

</Solution>
