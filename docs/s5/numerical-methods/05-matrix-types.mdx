## Diagonally Dominant Matrix

An $n \times n$ matrix $A$ is diagonally dominant **iff**

<Formula>

$$
|a_{ii}| \geq \sum_{j=1, j \neq i}^{n} |a_{ij}|
$$

</Formula>

holds for each $i = 1, 2, \dots, n$.

### Strictly Diagonally Dominant Matrix

$A$ is strictly diagonally dominant **iff** the inequality above is strict for each $i$. Non-singular.

Gaussian elimination on $Ax = b$ then proceeds without row or column interchanges, and the computation is stable with respect to round-off error growth.

## Positive Definite Matrix

See [Positive Definite Matrix](/s4/linear-algebra/hermitian#positive-definite-matrix) for the definition and eigenvalue characterization.

A leading principal submatrix $A_k$ of $A$ is the $k \times k$ block

<Formula>

$$
A_k = \begin{bmatrix} a_{11} & \cdots & a_{1k} \\ \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ a_{k1} & \cdots & a_{kk} \end{bmatrix}
$$

</Formula>

for some $1 \leq k \leq n$.

A symmetric matrix $A$ is positive definite **iff** every leading principal submatrix has a positive determinant.

A symmetric matrix $A$ is positive definite **iff** Gaussian elimination on $Ax = b$ can be performed without row interchanges with all pivot elements positive. The computation is then stable with respect to round-off error growth.

## Condition Number

For an invertible matrix $A$, the condition number relative to a chosen matrix norm is

<Formula>

$$
\kappa_{\lVert\cdot\rVert}(A) = \lVert A \rVert \lVert A^{-1} \rVert
$$

</Formula>

The condition number depends on the chosen norm. For the matrix norm induced by the vector $p$-norm, write

<Formula>

$$
\kappa_p(A) = \lVert A \rVert_p \lVert A^{-1} \rVert_p
$$

</Formula>

When the norm is understood, write $\kappa(A)$.

For a singular matrix, $\kappa(A) = \infty$. For every invertible matrix, $\kappa(A) \geq 1$.

The condition number quantifies the degree of ill-conditioning. A large condition number indicates that small relative changes in the input can produce large relative changes in the solution.

- Well-conditioned matrix  
  $\kappa(A)$ is close to $1$. Small relative changes in the input produce small relative changes in the solution.
- Ill-conditioned matrix  
  $\kappa(A)$ is large. Small relative changes in the input can produce large relative changes in the solution.

For $Ax = b$ and a perturbation $\delta b$ in $b$,

<Formula>

$$
\frac{\lVert \delta x \rVert}{\lVert x \rVert}
\leq
\kappa(A)\frac{\lVert \delta b \rVert}{\lVert b \rVert}
$$

</Formula>

For example,

<Formula>

$$
\begin{cases} x + y = 2 \\ x + 1.001y = 2 \end{cases}
\qquad
\begin{cases} x + y = 2 \\ x + 1.001y = 2.001 \end{cases}
$$

</Formula>

The left system has solution $x = 2$, $y = 0$. The right system, obtained by a $0.001$ change in one constant, has solution $x = 1$, $y = 1$. The coefficient matrix is ill-conditioned, with condition number $4004$.

For a symmetric positive definite matrix, the eigenvalues in the [spectrum](/s5/numerical-methods/norms#spectrum) are positive. The $2$-norm condition number is

<Formula>

$$
\kappa_2(A) = \frac{\lambda_{\max}(A)}{\lambda_{\min}(A)}
$$

</Formula>

## Convergent Matrix

An $n \times n$ matrix $A$ is convergent to $B$ **iff**

<Formula>

$$
\lim_{k \to \infty} A^k = B
$$

</Formula>

For a square matrix $A$, the following are equivalent:

- $A$ is convergent to $0$, i.e. $\lim_{k \to \infty} A^k = 0$
- $\lim_{k \to \infty} \lVert A^k \rVert_m = 0$ for some matrix norm $\lVert \cdot \rVert_m$
- $\rho(A) < 1$, where $\rho(A)$ is the [spectral radius](/s5/numerical-methods/norms#spectral-radius)

## Worked Example

Check whether

<Formula>

$$
A = \begin{pmatrix} 7 & 2 & 0 \\ 3 & 5 & -1 \\ 0 & 5 & -6 \end{pmatrix}
$$

</Formula>

is strictly diagonally dominant.

<Solution>

$$
|7| = 7 > 2 + 0 = 2
$$

$$
|5| = 5 > 3 + 1 = 4
$$

$$
|-6| = 6 > 0 + 5 = 5
$$

All 3 rows satisfy the strict inequality. $A$ is strictly diagonally dominant, hence non-singular.

</Solution>

Check whether

$$
A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 & 0 \\ -1 & 2 & -1 \\ 0 & -1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}
$$

is positive definite.

<Solution>

$A$ is symmetric. Its leading principal submatrices have determinants

$$
\det A_1 = 2, \qquad \det A_2 = 3, \qquad \det A_3 = 4
$$

All are positive. $A$ is positive definite.

</Solution>

Check whether

<Formula>

$$
B = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & -1 & 0 \\ -1 & 4 & -1 \\ 0 & -1 & 4 \end{pmatrix}
$$

</Formula>

is positive definite.

<Solution>

$B$ is symmetric. The leading principal submatrices have determinants

$$
\det B_1 = 4
$$

$$
\det B_2 = 4 \cdot 4 - (-1)(-1) = 15
$$

$$
\det B_3 = 4(16 - 1) + 1(-4 - 0) = 56
$$

All are positive. $B$ is positive definite.

</Solution>

Check whether

<Formula>

$$
C = \begin{pmatrix} 0.5 & 0 \\ 0.25 & 0.5 \end{pmatrix}
$$

</Formula>

is convergent.

<Solution>

$C$ is lower triangular, so its eigenvalues are the diagonal entries $0.5$ and $0.5$.

$$
\rho(C) = 0.5 < 1
$$

$C$ is convergent to $0$.

</Solution>
