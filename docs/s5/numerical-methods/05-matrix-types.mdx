## Diagonally Dominant Matrix

An $n \times n$ matrix $A$ is diagonally dominant **iff**

$$
|a_{ii}| \geq \sum_{j=1, j \neq i}^{n} |a_{ij}|
$$

holds for each $i = 1, 2, \dots, n$.

### Strictly Diagonally Dominant Matrix

$A$ is strictly diagonally dominant **iff** the inequality above is strict for each $i$. Non-singular.

Gaussian elimination on $Ax = b$ then proceeds without row or column interchanges, and the computation is stable with respect to round-off error growth.

## Positive Definite Matrix

See [Positive Definite Matrix](/s4/linear-algebra/hermitian#positive-definite-matrix) for the definition and eigenvalue characterization.

A leading principal submatrix $A_k$ of $A$ is the $k \times k$ block

$$
A_k = \begin{bmatrix} a_{11} & \cdots & a_{1k} \\ \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ a_{k1} & \cdots & a_{kk} \end{bmatrix}
$$

for some $1 \leq k \leq n$.

A symmetric matrix $A$ is positive definite **iff** every leading principal submatrix has a positive determinant.

A symmetric matrix $A$ is positive definite **iff** Gaussian elimination on $Ax = b$ can be performed without row interchanges with all pivot elements positive. The computation is then stable with respect to round-off error growth.

$$
A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 & 0 \\ -1 & 2 & -1 \\ 0 & -1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}
$$

is positive definite: its leading principal submatrices have determinants $2$, $3$, and $4$, all positive.

## Convergent Matrix

An $n \times n$ matrix $A$ is convergent to $B$ **iff**

$$
\lim_{k \to \infty} A^k = B
$$

For a square matrix $A$, the following are equivalent:

- $A$ is convergent to $0$, i.e. $\lim_{k \to \infty} A^k = 0$
- $\lim_{k \to \infty} \lVert A^k \rVert_m = 0$ for some matrix norm $\lVert \cdot \rVert_m$
- $\rho(A) < 1$

## Worked Example

Check whether

$$
A = \begin{pmatrix} 7 & 2 & 0 \\ 3 & 5 & -1 \\ 0 & 5 & -6 \end{pmatrix}
$$

is strictly diagonally dominant.

<Solution>

$$
|7| = 7 > 2 + 0 = 2
$$

$$
|5| = 5 > 3 + 1 = 4
$$

$$
|-6| = 6 > 0 + 5 = 5
$$

All 3 rows satisfy the strict inequality. $A$ is strictly diagonally dominant, hence non-singular.

</Solution>

Check whether

$$
B = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & -1 & 0 \\ -1 & 4 & -1 \\ 0 & -1 & 4 \end{pmatrix}
$$

is positive definite.

<Solution>

$B$ is symmetric. The leading principal submatrices have determinants

$$
\det B_1 = 4
$$

$$
\det B_2 = 4 \cdot 4 - (-1)(-1) = 15
$$

$$
\det B_3 = 4(16 - 1) + 1(-4 - 0) = 56
$$

All are positive. $B$ is positive definite.

</Solution>

Check whether

$$
C = \begin{pmatrix} 0.5 & 0 \\ 0.25 & 0.5 \end{pmatrix}
$$

is convergent.

<Solution>

$C$ is lower triangular, so its eigenvalues are the diagonal entries $0.5$ and $0.5$.

$$
\rho(C) = 0.5 < 1
$$

$C$ is convergent to $0$.

</Solution>
