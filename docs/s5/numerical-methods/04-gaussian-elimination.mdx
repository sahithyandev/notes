Gaussian elimination reduces $A\vec{x} = \vec{b}$ to [echelon form](/s1/mathematics/matrices/echelon-form) by [row operations](/s5/numerical-methods/equivalent-systems), then solves it by back substitution.

## Forward elimination

The pivot element of a column is the entry used to eliminate the other entries in that column.

Provided $a_{11} \neq 0$, eliminate $x_1$ from equations $2$ through $n$ using

$$
R_j - \frac{a_{j1}}{a_{11}} R_1 \to R_j, \quad j = 2, 3, \ldots, n
$$

More generally, eliminate $x_i$ below row $i$, for $i = 1, 2, \ldots, n-1$, using

$$
R_j - \frac{a_{ji}^{(i)}}{a_{ii}^{(i)}} R_i \to R_j, \quad j = i+1, i+2, \ldots, n
$$

provided $a_{ii}^{(i)} \neq 0$. This reduces $[A \mid \vec{b}]$ to upper triangular form

$$
\begin{pmatrix}
a_{11} & a_{12} & \cdots & a_{1n} & b_1^{(1)} \\
0 & a_{22}^{(1)} & \cdots & a_{2n} & b_2^{(2)} \\
\vdots & & \ddots & \vdots & \vdots \\
0 & 0 & \cdots & a_{nn}^{(n-1)} & b_n^{(n-1)}
\end{pmatrix}
$$

## Back substitution

Once $[A \mid \vec{b}]$ is upper triangular, each row has 1 fewer unknown than the row above it. The last row has only $x_n$

$$
x_n = \frac{b_n^{(n-1)}}{a_{nn}^{(n-1)}}
$$

Each remaining unknown is then found from the bottom up, substituting in the ones already solved

$$
x_i = \frac{b_i^{(i-1)} - \sum\limits_{j=i+1}^{n} a_{ij}^{(i-1)}x_j}{a_{ii}^{(i-1)}}, \quad i = n-1, n-2, \ldots, 1
$$

Here, $\sum_{j=i+1}^{n} a_{ij}^{(i-1)}x_j$ is the contribution of the already-solved unknowns $x_{i+1}, \ldots, x_n$ to row $i$.

## Pivoting

Pivoting is interchanging rows to place a useful entry in the pivot position. It only reorders rows, so the system produced is still equivalent to the original.

### Partial pivoting

At stage $i$, scan column $i$ from row $i$ to row $n$, and swap the row with the largest absolute entry into row $i$ before eliminating.

Using the largest available entry as the pivot keeps the multipliers $\frac{a_{ji}^{(i)}}{a_{ii}^{(i)}}$ at or below $1$ in size, which stops them from amplifying rounding error.

<Note>

Searching both rows and columns for the largest element is called complete pivoting. It's rarely used since switching columns changes the order of the $x_i$'s and adds significant complexity.

</Note>

### Scaled partial pivoting

Partial pivoting alone can still pick a poor pivot if rows are scaled very differently, since an entry can be large only because its whole row has large entries.

Scaled partial pivoting corrects for this. For candidate entry $a_{ji}^{(i)}$, compute the scale factor

$$
s_j = \max_k |a_{jk}|
$$

then choose the row maximizing $\frac{|a_{ji}^{(i)}|}{s_j}$ instead of the raw entry $|a_{ji}^{(i)}|$. This picks the pivot largest relative to its own row, not largest in absolute terms.

<Note>

If $s_j = 0$ for some $j$, the system has no unique solution since the entire $j$-th row is $0$.

</Note>

Apply scaled partial pivoting to

$$
\begin{aligned}
30.00x_1 + 591400x_2 &= 591700 \\
5.291x_1 - 6.130x_2 &= 46.78
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
s_1 = \max\{|30.00|, |591400|\} = 591400, \quad s_2 = \max\{|5.291|, |6.130|\} = 6.130
$$

$$
\frac{|a_{11}|}{s_1} = \frac{30}{591400} = 5.073 \times 10^{-5}, \quad \frac{|a_{21}|}{s_2} = \frac{5.291}{6.130} = 0.8631
$$

$\dfrac{|a_{21}|}{s_2}$ is larger, so exchange $R_1 \leftrightarrow R_2$. Gauss elimination on the reordered system gives $x_1 = 10.00$ and $x_2 = 1.000$.

## Pitfalls

### Zero pivot

If $a_{ii}^{(i)} = 0$ at any stage, the elimination formula divides by zero, even though the system may still have a unique solution.

<Note>

Pivoting fixes this by swapping in a row below with a non-zero entry in that column.

</Note>

### Small pivot

A pivot that is small rather than zero doesn't stop the algorithm, but dividing by it inflates the multipliers $\frac{a_{ji}^{(i)}}{a_{ii}^{(i)}}$. This amplifies rounding error already present in $a_{ji}^{(i)}$ and $b_j^{(i)}$, which then propagates through every later stage and into back substitution.

### Ill conditioning

A problem is ill-conditioned **iff** a small change in any of its elements causes a large change in its solution. Otherwise the problem is well-conditioned.

Round-off errors effectively change the elements of $A$ and $\vec{b}$ slightly. If the system is ill-conditioned, these small changes produce large errors in the solution.

<Note>

Ill-conditioned systems are inherently difficult to handle in the presence of rounding errors. When rounding errors are unavoidable, ill-conditioned systems must be avoided wherever possible.

</Note>

If $A$ is close to singular, small errors in the input data or in rounding get magnified into large errors in $\vec{x}$, even with pivoting. Pivoting controls error growth from the algorithm itself, but cannot fix a system that is inherently sensitive to small changes in its input.

Consider

$$
\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1.0001 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}x\\y\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}2\\2.0001\end{pmatrix}
$$

with solution $x = 1, y = 1$. Changing $b_2$ from $2.0001$ to $2.0002$, a change of $0.0001$, gives

$$
\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1.0001 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}x\\y\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}2\\2.0002\end{pmatrix}
$$

with solution $x = 0, y = 2$. A $0.0001$ change in $b_2$ moved $x$ by $1$ and $y$ by $1$. No amount of pivoting prevents this, since the coefficient matrix itself is nearly singular, $\det \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1.0001 \end{pmatrix} = 0.0001$.

#### Condition Number

A measure of the degree of ill-conditioning. Denoted as $\kappa(A)$. Defined as:

$$
\kappa(A) = \|A\| \, \|A^{-1}\|
$$

A large $\kappa(A)$ means $A$ is close to singular, and small errors in $\vec{b}$ or in rounding get magnified into large errors in $\vec{x}$.

$$
\begin{cases} x + y = 2 \\ x + 1.001y = 2 \end{cases}
\qquad
\begin{cases} x + y = 2 \\ x + 1.001y = 2.001 \end{cases}
$$

The left system has solution $x = 2$, $y = 0$. The right system, obtained by a $0.001$ change in one constant, has solution $x = 1$, $y = 1$. The coefficient matrix is ill-conditioned, with condition number $4004$.

### Cost

| Method              | Time     | Space    |
| ------------------- | -------- | -------- |
| Forward elimination | $O(n^3)$ | $O(n^2)$ |
| Back substitution   | $O(n^2)$ | $O(n^2)$ |

Space is $O(n^2)$ for both, for storing the augmented matrix $[A \mid \vec{b}]$. Elimination is done in place, so no extra storage proportional to $n^2$ is needed beyond this.

The $O(n^3)$ time cost of forward elimination makes Gaussian elimination unsuitable for repeatedly solving $A\vec{x} = \vec{b}$ for many different $\vec{b}$ with the same $A$.
