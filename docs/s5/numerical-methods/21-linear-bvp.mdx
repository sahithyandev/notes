A [boundary value problem](/s5/numerical-methods/boundary-value-problem) is linear **iff**

<Formula>

$$
y'' = p(x)y' + q(x)y + r(x), \quad a \leq x \leq b, \quad y(a) = \alpha,\ y(b) = \beta
$$

</Formula>

for continuous functions $p$, $q$, and $r$.

## Linear Shooting Method

Solves a linear BVP by combining solutions to 2 initial value problems.

A unique solution exists **if**

- $p$, $q$, and $r$ are continuous on $[a,b]$
- $q(x) > 0$ for $x \in [a,b]$

### Superposition Idea

If $y_1$ solves

<Formula>

$$
y_1'' = p(x)y_1' + q(x)y_1 + r(x)
$$

</Formula>

and $y_2$ solves the homogeneous equation

<Formula>

$$
y_2'' = p(x)y_2' + q(x)y_2
$$

</Formula>

then $y = y_1 + c\,y_2$ solves the ODE for any constant $c$.

### Auxiliary IVPs

Fix

<Formula>

$$
y_1(a) = \alpha,\ y_1'(a) = 0, \qquad y_2(a) = 0,\ y_2'(a) = 1
$$

</Formula>

Solve

<Formula>

$$
y_1'' = p(x)y_1' + q(x)y_1 + r(x), \quad y_1(a) = \alpha,\ y_1'(a) = 0
\\[8pt]
y_2'' = p(x)y_2' + q(x)y_2, \quad y_2(a) = 0,\ y_2'(a) = 1
$$

</Formula>

with [order 4 Runge-Kutta](/s5/numerical-methods/systems-of-ivps).

### Combining Solutions

Choose $c$ so that $y(b) = \beta$.

<Formula>

$$
\begin{aligned}
c &= \frac{\beta - y_1(b)}{y_2(b)} \\
y(x) &= y_1(x) + c\,y_2(x)
\end{aligned}
$$

</Formula>

## Linear Finite-Difference Method

Replace derivatives with centered differences at mesh points and solve the resulting linear system.

### Mesh Points

Partition $[a,b]$ into $N+1$ subintervals of length $h = \dfrac{b-a}{N+1}$.

<Formula>

$$
x_i = a + ih, \quad i = 0, 1, \ldots, N+1
$$

</Formula>

### Centered Difference Formulas

<Formula>

$$
y''(x_i) = \frac{y(x_{i+1}) - 2y(x_i) + y(x_{i-1})}{h^2} + O(h^2), \qquad y'(x_i) = \frac{y(x_{i+1}) - y(x_{i-1})}{2h} + O(h^2)
$$

</Formula>

### Linear Difference Equation

With $y(x_i) \approx w_i$, $w_0 = \alpha$, and $w_{N+1} = \beta$,

<Formula>

$$
\left(1 + \frac{h}{2}p(x_i)\right)w_{i-1} - \big(2 + h^2 q(x_i)\big)w_i + \left(1 - \frac{h}{2}p(x_i)\right)w_{i+1} = h^2 r(x_i)
$$

</Formula>

for $i = 1, \ldots, N$, with truncation error $O(h^2)$.

### Matrix Form

<Formula>

$$
Aw = b
$$

</Formula>

where $A$ is tridiagonal, with

<Formula>

$$
a_i = -1 - \frac{h}{2}p(x_i), \quad d_i = 2 + h^2q(x_i), \quad c_i = -1 + \frac{h}{2}p(x_i)
\\[8pt]
A = \begin{pmatrix} d_1 & c_1 & & \\ a_2 & d_2 & c_2 & \\ & \ddots & \ddots & \ddots \\ & & a_N & d_N \end{pmatrix}
\\[8pt]
w = \begin{pmatrix}w_1\\w_2\\\vdots\\w_N\end{pmatrix}, \quad b = \begin{pmatrix}-h^2r(x_1)-a_1\alpha\\-h^2r(x_2)\\\vdots\\-h^2r(x_N)-c_N\beta\end{pmatrix}
$$

</Formula>

Solve for $w$ with [LU decomposition](/s5/numerical-methods/lu-decomposition) or another linear solver.

If $p$, $q$, and $r$ are continuous on $[a,b]$ and $q(x) \geq 0$, the linear system has a unique solution whenever $h < \dfrac{2}{L}$, where $L = \max_{a \leq x \leq b}|p(x)|$.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1

Solve $y'' = y$, $0 \leq x \leq 1$, $y(0) = 0$, and $y(1) = 1$ with the linear shooting method.

<Solution>

$p = 0$, $q = 1$, and $r = 0$.

Use step size $h=0.25$. For the shooting method, solve the auxiliary IVPs with order 4 Runge-Kutta:

$$
y_1'' = y_1, \quad y_1(0) = 0,\ y_1'(0) = 0
$$

and

$$
y_2'' = y_2, \quad y_2(0) = 0,\ y_2'(0) = 1
$$

The first IVP has $y_1(x)=0$. Applying order 4 Runge-Kutta to the second IVP gives

$$
\begin{array}{c|cc}
x & y_2(x) & y_2'(x) \\\hline
0 & 0 & 1 \\
0.25 & 0.252604 & 1.031413 \\
0.50 & 0.521078 & 1.127621 \\
0.75 & 0.822289 & 1.294669 \\
1.00 & 1.175158 & 1.543052
\end{array}
$$

Therefore,

$$
c = \frac{1-y_1(1)}{y_2(1)} = \frac{1-0}{1.175158} = 0.8509
$$

Combining the auxiliary solutions gives

$$
y(x) = y_1(x) + c y_2(x) \approx 0.8509y_2(x)
$$

</Solution>

### Example 2

Solve $y'' = y+1$, $0 \leq x \leq 1$, $y(0) = 0$, and $y(1) = 1$ with the linear shooting method. Use order 4 Runge-Kutta with $h=0.25$.

<Solution>

Here $p=0$, $q=1$, and $r=1$. The auxiliary IVPs are

$$
y_1''=y_1+1, \quad y_1(0)=0,\ y_1'(0)=0
$$

and

$$
y_2''=y_2, \quad y_2(0)=0,\ y_2'(0)=1
$$

Order 4 Runge-Kutta gives

$$
y_1(1)\approx0.543052, \qquad y_2(1)\approx1.175158
$$

Therefore,

$$
c=\frac{1-y_1(1)}{y_2(1)}=\frac{1-0.543052}{1.175158}\approx0.38884
$$

The shooting approximation is

$$
y(x)\approx y_1(x)+0.38884y_2(x)
$$

</Solution>
