A linear system whose coefficient matrix has non-zero entries only on the main diagonal and the 2 adjacent diagonals.

<Formula>

$$
\begin{aligned}
b_1x_1 + c_1x_2 &= d_1 \\
a_2x_1 + b_2x_2 + c_2x_3 &= d_2 \\
&\ \vdots \\
a_{n-1}x_{n-2} + b_{n-1}x_{n-1} + c_{n-1}x_n &= d_{n-1} \\
a_nx_{n-1} + b_nx_n &= d_n
\end{aligned}
$$

<Symbols>
  <Sym s="a_i">subdiagonal entries</Sym>
  <Sym s="b_i">main diagonal entries</Sym>
  <Sym s="c_i">superdiagonal entries</Sym>
  <Sym s="d_i">right-hand-side entries</Sym>
</Symbols>

</Formula>

Its matrix form is

<Formula>

$$
\begin{pmatrix}
b_1 & c_1 & 0 & \cdots & 0 \\
a_2 & b_2 & c_2 & \ddots & \vdots \\
0 & a_3 & b_3 & \ddots & 0 \\
\vdots & \ddots & \ddots & \ddots & c_{n-1} \\
0 & \cdots & 0 & a_n & b_n
\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}x_1\\x_2\\\vdots\\x_n\end{pmatrix}
=
\begin{pmatrix}d_1\\d_2\\\vdots\\d_n\end{pmatrix}
$$

</Formula>

The missing entries are zero. The first subdiagonal entry $a_1$ and the last superdiagonal entry $c_n$ are not used.

## Crout Factorization

Crout factorization writes the coefficient matrix as

<Formula>

$$
A=LU,
$$

</Formula>

Here, $L$ is lower triangular and $U$ has unit diagonal. For a tridiagonal matrix,

<Formula>

$$
L=
\begin{pmatrix}
\ell_1 & 0 & \cdots & 0 \\
a_2 & \ell_2 & \ddots & \vdots \\
0 & a_3 & \ddots & 0 \\
\vdots & \ddots & \ddots & \ell_n
\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
U=
\begin{pmatrix}
1 & u_1 & 0 & \cdots & 0 \\
0 & 1 & u_2 & \ddots & \vdots \\
\vdots & \ddots & \ddots & \ddots & 0 \\
0 & \cdots & 0 & 1 & u_{n-1} \\
0 & \cdots & \cdots & 0 & 1
\end{pmatrix}
$$

</Formula>

The factor entries are computed by

<Formula>

$$
\begin{aligned}
\ell_1 &= b_1, & u_1 &= \frac{c_1}{\ell_1}, \\
\ell_i &= b_i-a_i u_{i-1}, & u_i &= \frac{c_i}{\ell_i},
\quad i=2,\ldots,n-1, \\
\ell_n &= b_n-a_n u_{n-1}.
\end{aligned}
$$

</Formula>

The subdiagonal entries of $L$ are the original entries $a_i$. The factorization requires every pivot $\ell_i$ to be non-zero.

### Triangular Solves

After $A=LU$, solve $A\vec{x}=\vec{d}$ by solving $L\vec{y}=\vec{d}$ and then $U\vec{x}=\vec{y}$.

<Formula>

$$
\begin{aligned}
y_1 &= \frac{d_1}{\ell_1}, \\
y_i &= \frac{d_i-a_i y_{i-1}}{\ell_i},
\quad i=2,\ldots,n.
\end{aligned}
$$

</Formula>

Back substitution uses

<Formula>

$$
\begin{aligned}
x_n &= y_n, \\
x_i &= y_i-u_i x_{i+1},
\quad i=n-1,n-2,\ldots,1.
\end{aligned}
$$

</Formula>

The factorization, forward substitution, and back substitution each require $O(n)$ operations. The factors and working vectors require $O(n)$ storage.

## Applications

Tridiagonal systems occur when a discretized equation couples each unknown only to its immediate neighbours.

- Finite-difference discretization of 1-dimensional boundary-value problems.
- Cubic spline interpolation.
- One-dimensional diffusion and heat equations.
- Electrical ladder networks.

## Worked Example

Solve the tridiagonal system using Crout factorization.

<Formula>

$$
\begin{aligned}
2x_1-x_2 &= 1 \\
-x_1+2x_2-x_3 &= 0 \\
-x_2+2x_3-x_4 &= 0 \\
-x_3+2x_4 &= 1.
\end{aligned}
$$

</Formula>

<Solution>

The entries are

$$
a_2=a_3=a_4=-1,
\quad b_1=b_2=b_3=b_4=2,
\quad c_1=c_2=c_3=-1,
\quad \vec{d}=(1,0,0,1)^T
$$

Crout factorization gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
\ell_1 &= 2, & u_1 &= -\frac12, \\
\ell_2 &= 2-(-1)\left(-\frac12\right)=\frac32, & u_2 &= -\frac23, \\
\ell_3 &= 2-(-1)\left(-\frac23\right)=\frac43, & u_3 &= -\frac34, \\
\ell_4 &= 2-(-1)\left(-\frac34\right)=\frac54.
\end{aligned}
$$

Forward substitution gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
y_1 &= \frac12, \\
y_2 &= \frac{0-(-1)(1/2)}{3/2}=\frac13, \\
y_3 &= \frac{0-(-1)(1/3)}{4/3}=\frac14, \\
y_4 &= \frac{1-(-1)(1/4)}{5/4}=1.
\end{aligned}
$$

Back substitution gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
x_4 &= 1, \\
x_3 &= \frac14-\left(-\frac34\right)(1)=1, \\
x_2 &= \frac13-\left(-\frac23\right)(1)=1, \\
x_1 &= \frac12-\left(-\frac12\right)(1)=1.
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore,

$$
\vec{x}=(1,1,1,1)^T
$$

</Solution>
