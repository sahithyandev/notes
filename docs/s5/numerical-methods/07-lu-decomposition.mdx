The process of factorizing a square matrix into 2 [triangular matrices](/s1/mathematics/matrices/introduction#triangular-matrix): a lower triangular matrix ($L$) and an upper triangular matrix ($U$).
Introduced by Alan Turing in 1948.

$$
A = LU
$$

## Finding $L$ and $U$ Without Pivoting

### Using Gaussian Elimination

Can be used when [Gaussian elimination](/s5/numerical-methods/gaussian-elimination) can be performed on $A\vec{x} = \vec{b}$ without row interchanges, $A$ factors as $A = LU$, where

$$
m_{ji} = \frac{a_{ji}^{(i)}}{a_{ii}^{(i)}}
$$

is the multiplier used to eliminate entry $(j, i)$ at step $i$, $U$ is the row-echelon form produced by elimination, and $L$ collects the multipliers:

$$
U = \begin{pmatrix}
a_{11}^{(1)} & a_{12}^{(1)} & \cdots & a_{1n}^{(1)} \\
0 & a_{22}^{(2)} & \cdots & a_{2n}^{(2)} \\
\vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\
0 & \cdots & \cdots & a_{nn}^{(n)} \end{pmatrix}

\; \text{and} \;

L = \begin{pmatrix}
1 & 0 & \cdots & 0 \\
m_{21} & 1 & \cdots & \vdots \\
\vdots & \vdots & \ddots & 0 \\
m_{n1} & \cdots & m_{n,n-1} & 1 \end{pmatrix}
$$

$L$ and $U$ are produced together in 1 pass of elimination.

- For $i = 1, \ldots, n-1$, use row $i$ as the pivot row.
- For each row $j > i$, compute $m_{ji} = a_{ji}^{(i)} / a_{ii}^{(i)}$.
- Eliminate $a_{ji}$ with $\text{row}_j \leftarrow \text{row}_j - m_{ji} \cdot \text{row}_i$.
- Place $m_{ji}$ at position $(j, i)$ of $L$.

$U$ is the result of elimination. $L$ is the record of multipliers used to produce it.

### Direct Computation Method

$L$ and $U$ can also be computed directly by equating entries of $A$ with entries of the product $LU$.

- For $i = 1, \ldots, n$, set $l_{ii} = 1$ or $u_{ii} = 1$.
- Compute $l_{11} u_{11} = a_{11}$.
- For $j = 2, \ldots, n$, compute

$$
u_{1j} = \frac{a_{1j}}{l_{11}}, \quad l_{j1} = \frac{a_{j1}}{u_{11}}
$$

- For $i = 2, \ldots, n-1$:
  - Compute $l_{ii} u_{ii} = a_{ii} - \sum_{k=1}^{i-1} l_{ik} u_{ki}$.
    - $a_{ii}$  
      Diagonal entry of $A$ at row $i$.
    - $\sum_{k=1}^{i-1} l_{ik} u_{ki}$  
      Contribution already accounted for by the first $i-1$ rows and columns of $L$ and $U$.
    - $l_{ii} u_{ii}$  
      Remainder left for the diagonal entries of $L$ and $U$ at step $i$, split by the fixed value from step 1.
  - For $j = i+1, \ldots, n$, compute

$$
u_{ij} = \frac{1}{l_{ii}} \left[ a_{ij} - \sum_{k=1}^{i-1} l_{ik} u_{kj} \right], \quad
l_{ji} = \frac{1}{u_{ii}} \left[ a_{ji} - \sum_{k=1}^{i-1} l_{jk} u_{ki} \right]
$$

<Note>

Setting $l_{ii} = 1$ for all $i$ gives Doolittle's method. Setting $u_{ii} = 1$ for all $i$ gives Crout's method.

</Note>

## Pivoting

LU decomposition can fail when the top-left entry of $A$ is $0$ or small compared to other entries.

Pivoting rearranges the rows and/or columns of $A$ to place a larger element in the top-left position, mitigating this. Multiple pivoting algorithms exist.

## Applications

- Solving $A\vec{x} = \vec{b}$, used for finding current in a circuit and solving discrete dynamical systems.
- Finding the inverse of a matrix.
- Finding the determinant of a matrix.

Applicable whenever a problem can be modeled in matrix form, since triangular matrices are easy to compute with.

### Solving $A\vec{x} = \vec{b}$

Suppose $A = LU$ and $A\vec{x} = LU\vec{x} = \vec{b}$ must be solved.

- Set $\vec{w} = U\vec{x}$.
- Solve $L\vec{w} = \vec{b}$ for $\vec{w}$ by forward substitution, since $L$ is lower triangular.
- Solve $U\vec{x} = \vec{w}$ for $\vec{x}$ by back substitution, since $U$ is upper triangular. This $\vec{x}$ solves the original system.

This uses $L$ to perform row operations on $U$ to solve the system.
