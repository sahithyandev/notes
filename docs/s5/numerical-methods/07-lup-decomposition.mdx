The LUP decomposition of an $n \times n$ matrix $A$ is the triple $(L, U, P)$ satisfying

<Formula>

$$
PA = LU
$$

<Symbols>
  <Sym s="L">
    unit lower triangular, $n \times n$ with all diagonal entries $1$
  </Sym>
  <Sym s="U">upper triangular, $n \times n$</Sym>
  <Sym s="P">permutation matrix, $n \times n$</Sym>
</Symbols>

</Formula>

## Properties

- $P$ reorders the rows of $A$ as in [partial pivoting](/s5/numerical-methods/gaussian-elimination#partial-pivoting).
- Exists for any nonsingular $A$.
- Not unique.
- More robust than LU without pivoting, at about the same cost.

## Comparison with LU Decomposition

The [LU decomposition](/s5/numerical-methods/lu-decomposition) factors $A$ as $A = LU$, without row interchanges. LUP selects the pivot row by size.

- Existence  
  A zero pivot stops the elimination. LUP always factors a nonsingular $A$.
- Stability  
  A small pivot gives large multipliers and amplifies rounding error. Partial pivoting keeps every multiplier at most $1$ in size.
- Cost  
  Factorization costs $O(n^3)$ for both. Each solve costs $O(n^2)$. LUP adds the row swaps and the permutation of $\vec{b}$.

### When to Use Which

LUP is the default for a general dense matrix. The pivot sizes are unknown in advance.

Use LU without pivoting when the pivots are safe by construction.

- Diagonally dominant $A$  
  Every pivot is nonzero without a swap.
- Symmetric positive definite $A$  
  Every leading principal minor is positive.
- Banded or sparse $A$  
  Row interchanges would add fill-in.

## Solving $A\vec{x} = \vec{b}$

$PA\vec{x} = P\vec{b}$ becomes $LU\vec{x} = P\vec{b}$.

- Solve $L\vec{y} = P\vec{b}$ by forward substitution.
- Solve $U\vec{x} = \vec{y}$ by [back substitution](/s5/numerical-methods/gaussian-elimination#back-substitution).

## Recursive Leading-Row-Column Algorithm

Set $A^{(0)} = A$. At recursion depth $k$, factor the current $(n-k) \times (n-k)$ matrix $A^{(k)}$.

Let $S^{(k)}$ swap the row with the largest entry in magnitude in the first column of $A^{(k)}$ into the first row. Partition the swapped matrix $B^{(k)} = S^{(k)}A^{(k)}$ as

<Formula>

$$
B^{(k)} = \begin{pmatrix} \alpha^{(k)} & \left(r^{(k)}\right)^T \\ c^{(k)} & C^{(k)} \end{pmatrix}
$$

<Symbols>
  <Sym s="A^{(k)}">matrix entering recursion depth $k$</Sym>
  <Sym s="B^{(k)}">matrix after the row swap</Sym>
  <Sym s="\alpha^{(k)}">pivot</Sym>
  <Sym s="\left(r^{(k)}\right)^T">rest of the pivot row</Sym>
  <Sym s="c^{(k)}">rest of the pivot column</Sym>
  <Sym s="C^{(k)}">trailing block</Sym>
  <Sym s="\ell^{(k)}">multiplier vector</Sym>
  <Sym s="S^{(k)}">row swap at recursion depth $k$</Sym>
</Symbols>

</Formula>

### Schur Complement

Define the multiplier vector and the next recursive matrix as

<Formula>

$$
\begin{aligned}
\ell^{(k)} &= \frac{c^{(k)}}{\alpha^{(k)}} \\
A^{(k+1)} &= C^{(k)} - \ell^{(k)}\left(r^{(k)}\right)^T
\end{aligned}
$$

</Formula>

$A^{(k+1)}$ is the Schur complement of $α^{(k)}$ in $B^{(k)}$.

### Base Case

When $A^{(k)}$ is $1 \times 1$, set

<Formula>

$$
P^{(k)} = L^{(k)} = (1), \qquad U^{(k)} = A^{(k)}
$$

</Formula>

### Recursive Step

For a matrix larger than $1 \times 1$:

- Form $B^{(k)} = S^{(k)}A^{(k)}$ and partition it.
- Compute $\ell^{(k)}$ and $A^{(k+1)}$.
- Recurse on $A^{(k+1)}$ to get $P^{(k+1)}A^{(k+1)} = L^{(k+1)}U^{(k+1)}$.
- Return

<Formula>

$$
\begin{aligned}
P^{(k)} &= \begin{pmatrix} 1 & \vec{0}^T \\ \vec{0} & P^{(k+1)} \end{pmatrix}S^{(k)} \\
L^{(k)} &= \begin{pmatrix} 1 & \vec{0}^T \\ P^{(k+1)}\ell^{(k)} & L^{(k+1)} \end{pmatrix} \\
U^{(k)} &= \begin{pmatrix} \alpha^{(k)} & \left(r^{(k)}\right)^T \\ \vec{0} & U^{(k+1)} \end{pmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

</Formula>

The required decomposition is $P = P^{(0)}$, $L = L^{(0)}$, $U = U^{(0)}$.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1

Find $L$, $U$, $P$ for

<Formula>

$$
A = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 5 & 5 \\ 2 & 9 & 0 \\ 6 & 8 & 8 \end{pmatrix}
$$

</Formula>

<Solution>

Set $A^{(0)} = A$. At depth $0$, column $1$ is $(0, 2, 6)^T$. The pivot is $6$ in row $3$. Let $S^{(0)}$ swap rows $1$ and $3$.

$$
S^{(0)} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}
$$

$$
B^{(0)} = S^{(0)}A^{(0)} = \begin{pmatrix} 6 & 8 & 8 \\ 2 & 9 & 0 \\ 0 & 5 & 5 \end{pmatrix}
$$

$$
\alpha^{(0)} = 6, \quad \left(r^{(0)}\right)^T = (8 \enspace 8), \quad c^{(0)} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}, \quad C^{(0)} = \begin{pmatrix} 9 & 0 \\ 5 & 5 \end{pmatrix}
$$

$$
\ell^{(0)} = \begin{pmatrix} 1/3 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}, \quad
A^{(1)} = C^{(0)} - \ell^{(0)}\left(r^{(0)}\right)^T = \begin{pmatrix} 19/3 & -8/3 \\ 5 & 5 \end{pmatrix}
$$

At depth $1$, column $1$ of $A^{(1)}$ is $(19/3, 5)^T$. The pivot is already in row $1$, so $S^{(1)} = I_2$.

$$
B^{(1)} = A^{(1)}, \quad \alpha^{(1)} = \frac{19}{3}, \quad \left(r^{(1)}\right)^T = \left(-\frac{8}{3}\right), \quad c^{(1)} = (5), \quad C^{(1)} = (5)
$$

$$
\ell^{(1)} = \frac{15}{19}, \quad
A^{(2)} = C^{(1)} - \ell^{(1)}\left(r^{(1)}\right)^T = \frac{135}{19}
$$

At depth $2$, $A^{(2)}$ is $1 \times 1$. Therefore $P^{(2)} = L^{(2)} = (1)$ and $U^{(2)} = (135/19)$.

Returning from depth $1$

$$
P^{(1)} = I_2, \quad L^{(1)} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 15/19 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \quad U^{(1)} = \begin{pmatrix} 19/3 & -8/3 \\ 0 & 135/19 \end{pmatrix}
$$

Returning from depth $0$

$$
P = P^{(0)} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \quad
L = L^{(0)} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 1/3 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 15/19 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \quad
U = U^{(0)} = \begin{pmatrix} 6 & 8 & 8 \\ 0 & 19/3 & -8/3 \\ 0 & 0 & 135/19 \end{pmatrix}
$$

Check, row 3 of $LU$

$$
\tfrac{15}{19}\left(0, \tfrac{19}{3}, -\tfrac{8}{3}\right) + \left(0, 0, \tfrac{135}{19}\right) = (0, 5, 5)
$$

</Solution>

### Example 2

Using $P$, $L$, $U$ from Example 1, solve $A\vec{x} = \vec{b}$ for $\vec{b} = (10, 11, 22)^T$.

<Solution>

$$
P\vec{b} = (22, 11, 10)^T
$$

Forward substitution, $L\vec{y} = P\vec{b}$

$$
y_1 = 22
$$

$$
y_2 = 11 - \frac{1}{3}(22) = \frac{11}{3}
$$

$$
y_3 = 10 - \frac{15}{19}\cdot\frac{11}{3} = \frac{135}{19}
$$

Back substitution, $U\vec{x} = \vec{y}$

$$
x_3 = \frac{135/19}{135/19} = 1
$$

$$
x_2 = \frac{11/3 + (8/3)(1)}{19/3} = 1
$$

$$
x_1 = \frac{22 - 8(1) - 8(1)}{6} = 1
$$

$$
\vec{x} = (1, 1, 1)^T
$$

</Solution>
