An $m$th-order system of first-order [IVPs](/s5/numerical-methods/initial-value-problems) is

$$
\begin{aligned}
u_1'(t) &= f_1(t, u_1, u_2, \ldots, u_m) \\
&\ \ \vdots \\
u_m'(t) &= f_m(t, u_1, u_2, \ldots, u_m)
\end{aligned}
$$

for $a \leq t \leq b$, with $u_1(a) = \alpha_1, \ldots, u_m(a) = \alpha_m$. The goal is to approximate $u_1(t), \ldots, u_m(t)$.

## Lipschitz Condition for Systems

$f(t, u_1, \ldots, u_m)$ on

$$
D = \{ (t, u_1, \ldots, u_m) : a \leq t \leq b,\ -\infty < u_i < \infty \}
$$

satisfies a Lipschitz condition in $u_1, \ldots, u_m$ **iff** a constant $L > 0$ exists with

$$
|f(t, u_1, \ldots, u_m) - f(t, z_1, \ldots, z_m)| \leq L \sum_{j=1}^{m} |u_j - z_j|
$$

for all $(t, u_1, \ldots, u_m), (t, z_1, \ldots, z_m) \in D$.

Sufficient test: $f$ and each partial derivative $\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial u_i}$ are continuous on $D$ with $\left| \dfrac{\partial f}{\partial u_i} \right| \leq L$ for each $i = 1, \ldots, m$.

### Existence and Uniqueness

If each $f_i$ is continuous and satisfies a Lipschitz condition on $D$, the system has a unique solution $u_1(t), \ldots, u_m(t)$ on $[a, b]$.

## Order 4 Runge-Kutta for Systems

Superscript $(j)$ marks quantities for $u_j$. Mesh points are $t_i = a + ih$ with $h = \dfrac{b - a}{N}$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
w_0^{(j)} &= \alpha_j \\
k_1^{(j)} &= h\, f_j\big(t_i,\ w_i^{(1)}, \ldots, w_i^{(m)}\big) \\
k_2^{(j)} &= h\, f_j\left( t_i + \tfrac{h}{2},\ w_i^{(1)} + \tfrac{1}{2} k_1^{(1)}, \ldots, w_i^{(m)} + \tfrac{1}{2} k_1^{(m)} \right) \\
k_3^{(j)} &= h\, f_j\left( t_i + \tfrac{h}{2},\ w_i^{(1)} + \tfrac{1}{2} k_2^{(1)}, \ldots, w_i^{(m)} + \tfrac{1}{2} k_2^{(m)} \right) \\
k_4^{(j)} &= h\, f_j\left( t_{i+1},\ w_i^{(1)} + k_3^{(1)}, \ldots, w_i^{(m)} + k_3^{(m)} \right) \\
w_{i+1}^{(j)} &= w_i^{(j)} + \tfrac{1}{6}\left( k_1^{(j)} + 2 k_2^{(j)} + 2 k_3^{(j)} + k_4^{(j)} \right)
\end{aligned}
$$

for $i = 0, \ldots, N-1$ and $j = 1, \ldots, m$. All $k_1^{(j)}$ are computed before any $k_2^{(j)}$, and so on. Then $u_j(t_i) \approx w_i^{(j)}$.

## Reducing a Higher-Order IVP

An $m$th-order IVP

$$
y^{(m)}(t) = f\big(t, y, y', \ldots, y^{(m-1)}\big), \quad a \leq t \leq b
$$

with $y(a) = \alpha_1, y'(a) = \alpha_2, \ldots, y^{(m-1)}(a) = \alpha_m$ becomes a first-order system by setting $u_1 = y, u_2 = y', \ldots, u_m = y^{(m-1)}$

$$
\begin{aligned}
u_1' &= u_2 \\
u_2' &= u_3 \\
&\ \ \vdots \\
u_{m-1}' &= u_m \\
u_m' &= f(t, u_1, u_2, \ldots, u_m)
\end{aligned}
$$

with $u_1(a) = \alpha_1, \ldots, u_m(a) = \alpha_m$. Solving the system by order 4 Runge-Kutta, $w_i^{(1)}$ approximates $y(t_i)$.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1

Reduce

$$
y'' - 2y' + 2y = e^{2t}\sin t, \quad 0 \leq t \leq 1, \quad y(0) = -0.4,\ y'(0) = -0.6
$$

to a first-order system, then take 1 step of order 4 Runge-Kutta with $h = 0.5$.

<Solution>

Set $u_1 = y$, $u_2 = y'$

$$
\begin{aligned}
u_1' &= u_2 \\
u_2' &= 2 u_2 - 2 u_1 + e^{2t}\sin t
\end{aligned}
$$

with $u_1(0) = -0.4$, $u_2(0) = -0.6$. So $f_1 = u_2$ and $f_2 = 2u_2 - 2u_1 + e^{2t}\sin t$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
k_1^{(1)} &= 0.5(-0.6) = -0.3 \\
k_1^{(2)} &= 0.5\, f_2(0, -0.4, -0.6) = -0.2
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
k_2^{(1)} &= 0.5(-0.6 - 0.1) = -0.35 \\
k_2^{(2)} &= 0.5\, f_2(0.25, -0.55, -0.7) = 0.0540
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
k_3^{(1)} &= 0.5(-0.6 + 0.0270) = -0.2865 \\
k_3^{(2)} &= 0.5\, f_2(0.25, -0.575, -0.5730) = 0.2059
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
k_4^{(1)} &= 0.5(-0.6 + 0.2059) = -0.1970 \\
k_4^{(2)} &= 0.5\, f_2(0.5, -0.6865, -0.3941) = 0.9440
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
w_1^{(1)} = -0.4 + \tfrac{1}{6}(-0.3 - 0.7 - 0.5730 - 0.1970) = -0.6950
$$

$$
w_1^{(2)} = -0.6 + \tfrac{1}{6}(-0.2 + 0.1080 + 0.4118 + 0.9440) = -0.3894
$$

$w_1^{(1)}$ approximates $y(0.5)$. The exact value is $-0.6936$.

</Solution>

### Example 2

Write $y''' = y'' - 2y' + y + t$ with $y(0) = 1$, $y'(0) = 0$, $y''(0) = 2$ as a first-order system.

<Solution>

Set $u_1 = y$, $u_2 = y'$, $u_3 = y''$

$$
\begin{aligned}
u_1' &= u_2 \\
u_2' &= u_3 \\
u_3' &= u_3 - 2u_2 + u_1 + t
\end{aligned}
$$

with $u_1(0) = 1$, $u_2(0) = 0$, $u_3(0) = 2$.

</Solution>
