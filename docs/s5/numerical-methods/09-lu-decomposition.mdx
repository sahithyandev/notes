The LU decomposition of a square matrix $A$ is a factorization $A = LU$, where $L$ is lower triangular and $U$ is upper triangular. Alan Turing introduced it in 1948.

<Note>

$L$ lower triangular means all entries above the main diagonal are $0$. $U$ upper triangular means all entries below the main diagonal are $0$.

</Note>

## Solving linear systems with LU

To solve $A\vec{x} = LU\vec{x} = \vec{b}$:

- Set $\vec{w} = U\vec{x}$
- Solve $L\vec{w} = \vec{b}$ for $\vec{w}$ by forward substitution
- Solve $U\vec{x} = \vec{w}$ for $\vec{x}$ by back substitution

## Finding $L$ and $U$ without pivoting

If Gauss elimination can be performed on $A\vec{x} = \vec{b}$ without row interchanges, $A$ factors as $A = LU$, where

$$
m_{ji} = \frac{a_{ji}^{(i)}}{a_{ii}^{(i)}}
$$

gives the entries of $L$ below the diagonal, and $U$ is the row-echelon form produced by elimination.

<Note>

Taking $l_{ii} = 1$ for each $i$ gives a **Doolittle** decomposition. Taking $u_{ii} = 1$ instead gives a **Crout** decomposition.

LU decomposition fails when the top-left entry of $A$, or of a sub-matrix encountered during elimination, is $0$ or small compared to other entries. Pivoting mitigates this by rearranging rows to place a larger element in that position, giving an [LUP decomposition](/s5/numerical-methods/lup-decomposition).

</Note>

Examples:

Solve

$$
\begin{aligned}
6x + 18y + 3z &= 3 \\
2x + 12y &= 19 \\
4x + 15y + 3z &= 0
\end{aligned}
$$

with $A = LU$, where

$$
L = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 6 & 0 \\ 2 & 3 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad U = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 6 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}
$$

Solving $L\vec{w} = (3, 19, 0)^T$ by forward substitution gives $\vec{w} = (1, 3, -11)^T$.

Solving $U\vec{x} = \vec{w}$ by back substitution gives $z = -11$, $y = 3$, $x = -6$.
