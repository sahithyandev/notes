If $f(z)$ is [analytic](/s5/calculus/complex-functions/analytic-functions) at $z = \alpha$, the series

$$
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{f^{(n)}(\alpha)}{n!}(z-\alpha)^n = f(\alpha) + f'(\alpha)(z-\alpha) + \frac{f''(\alpha)}{2!}(z-\alpha)^2 + \cdots
$$

is called the Taylor series of $f(z)$ centered at $\alpha$. When $\alpha = 0$, it is called the Maclaurin series of $f(z)$.

## Taylor's Theorem

Let $f$ be analytic in a domain $G$, and let the disk $D_R(\alpha)$ be contained in $G$. Then the Taylor series of $f$ centered at $\alpha$ converges to $f(z)$ for all $z \in D_R(\alpha)$.

$$
f(z) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{f^{(n)}(\alpha)}{n!}(z-\alpha)^n, \quad z \in D_R(\alpha)
$$

The convergence is uniform on every closed disk $\{z : |z-\alpha| \le r\}$ with $0 < r < R$.

## Radius from Singular Points

Let $f$ be analytic in a domain $G$ containing $\alpha$. Let $z_0$ be a nearest singular point of $f$ to $\alpha$, and let $R = |z_0 - \alpha|$.

- The Taylor series of $f$ centered at $\alpha$ converges to $f(z)$ for each $z \in D_R(\alpha)$.
- No disk centered at $\alpha$ with a radius larger than $R$ can be a disk on which the series represents $f$.

## Uniqueness

Suppose

$$
f(z) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} a_n (z-\alpha)^n = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} b_n (z-\alpha)^n
$$

in some disk $D_r(\alpha)$ with $r > 0$. Then $a_n = b_n$ for each $n \in \mathbb{N} \cup \{0\}$.

Any power series representation of $f(z)$ in a disk centered at $\alpha$ is automatically the Taylor series of $f$ centered at $\alpha$.

<Note title="Proof Hint">

- Set $z = \alpha$ to get $a_0 = b_0$
- Differentiate term by term
- Set $z = \alpha$ again to get $a_1 = b_1$
- Repeat for each $n$

</Note>

Examples:

- $\frac{1}{(1-z)^2} = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} (n+1) z^n$ is valid for $z \in D_1(0)$
- $\sin z = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^n z^{2n+1}}{(2n+1)!}$

## Worked Examples

### Maclaurin Series of Sine

Find the Maclaurin series of $\sin z$.

The derivatives at $0$ cycle through $\sin z, \cos z, -\sin z, -\cos z$, so

$$
f(0) = 0, \quad f'(0) = 1, \quad f''(0) = 0, \quad f'''(0) = -1, \quad \ldots
$$

Only odd powers survive.

$$
\sin z = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^n z^{2n+1}}{(2n+1)!}
$$

$\sin z$ is entire, so the series is valid for all $z$.

### Series by Differentiation

Show that $\frac{1}{(1-z)^2} = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} (n+1) z^n$ for $z \in D_1(0)$.

Start with the geometric series.

$$
\frac{1}{1-z} = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} z^n, \quad |z| < 1
$$

Differentiate both sides term by term.

$$
\frac{1}{(1-z)^2} = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} n z^{n-1} = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} (n+1) z^n
$$

The only singular point is $z = 1$, so $R = 1$.

### Series Centered at a Nonzero Point

Find the Taylor series of $\frac{1}{z}$ centered at $\alpha = 1$ and its disk of validity.

Write $z = 1 + (z-1)$.

$$
\frac{1}{z} = \frac{1}{1 + (z-1)} = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} (-1)^n (z-1)^n
$$

The geometric series converges for $|z-1| < 1$. The nearest singular point is $0$, so $R = |0 - 1| = 1$ and the disk is $D_1(1)$.

### Series Valid in a Disk

Find the Taylor series of $\frac{1}{z+2}$ centered at $0$ and its disk of validity.

$$
\frac{1}{z+2} = \frac{1}{2} \cdot \frac{1}{1 + \frac{z}{2}} = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^n z^n}{2^{n+1}}
$$

The nearest singular point is $-2$, so $R = 2$ and the disk is $D_2(0)$.

### Coefficients from Uniqueness

Find $a_0, \ldots, a_4$ if $\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} a_n z^n = e^z + z^2$ near $0$.

By uniqueness, the $a_n$ are the Taylor coefficients of $e^z + z^2$.

$$
e^z + z^2 = 1 + z + \left(\frac{1}{2} + 1\right) z^2 + \frac{z^3}{6} + \frac{z^4}{24} + \cdots
$$

$$
a_0 = 1, \quad a_1 = 1, \quad a_2 = \frac{3}{2}, \quad a_3 = \frac{1}{6}, \quad a_4 = \frac{1}{24}
$$

### Polynomial Times Cosine

Find the Maclaurin series of $z^2 \cos z$ and its radius of convergence.

$$
z^2 \cos z = z^2 \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^n z^{2n}}{(2n)!} = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^n z^{2n+2}}{(2n)!}
$$

$z^2 \cos z$ is entire, so $R = \infty$.
