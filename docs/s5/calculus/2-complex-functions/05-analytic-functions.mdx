If $f$ is analytic on an open set $G$, $f'$ is continuous on $G$.

Let $u$ and $v$ be real-valued functions with continuous first-order partial derivatives on an open set $U \subseteq \mathbb{C}$, and define $f(z) = u(x,y)+iv(x,y)$, $z = x+iy$. Then $f$ is analytic in $U$ **iff** $u$ and $v$ satisfy the [Cauchy-Riemann equations](/s5/calculus/complex-functions/derivatives#polar-cauchy-riemann-equations) throughout $U$.

## Properties

Let $f$ be analytic on a domain $D$.

- $f'$ is analytic on $D$. So $f$ is infinitely differentiable on $D$.
- $\operatorname{Re} f$ and $\operatorname{Im} f$ are harmonic on $D$.
- If $f'(z) = 0$ throughout $D$, then $f$ is constant on $D$.
- If $|f|$ is constant on $D$, then $f$ is constant on $D$.
- If $\operatorname{Re} f$ is constant on $D$, or $\operatorname{Im} f$ is constant on $D$, then $f$ is constant on $D$.
- If $f$ is not constant on $D$, its zeros are isolated.

<Proof label="Proof Hint">

For $|f|$ constant:

- If $|f| = 0$, then $f = 0$
- Otherwise write $|f|^2 = u^2+v^2 = c$ with $c > 0$
- Differentiate in $x$ and $y$, apply the Cauchy-Riemann equations
- Get $u_x = u_y = v_x = v_y = 0$, so $f' = 0$

</Proof>

Let $f$ and $g$ be analytic in a domain $D$.

- $f+g$, $f-g$, and $fg$ are analytic in $D$.
- $f/g$ is analytic in $D$ provided $g$ does not vanish at any point of $D$.
- If $f$ is analytic in $D$ and $g$ is analytic on a domain containing the image of $D$ under $f$, then $g \circ f$ is analytic in $D$, and

$$
\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}z}\,g(f(z)) = g'(f(z))\,f'(z)
$$

## Singular Point

$z_0$ is a singular point (singularity) of $f$ **iff** $f$ fails to be analytic at $z_0$ **and** $f$ is analytic at some point in every neighborhood of $z_0$.

### Isolated Singularity

$z_0$ is an isolated singularity of $f$ **iff** $f$ is analytic on some punctured disc $D_r(z_0) \setminus \{z_0\}$ but not analytic at $z_0$. Equivalently, $z_0$ is a singular point and some punctured disc around $z_0$ contains no other singular point of $f$.

Examples:

- $z = 0$ is an isolated singularity of $f(z) = 1/z$.
- $z = i$ and $z = -i$ are isolated singularities of $f(z) = 1/(z^2+1)$.
- Every $z = n\pi$, $n \in \mathbb{Z}$, is an isolated singularity of $f(z) = \cot z$.

### Non-Isolated Singularity

$z_0$ is a non-isolated singularity of $f$ **iff** $z_0$ is a singular point and every punctured disc $D_r(z_0) \setminus \{z_0\}$ contains at least one other singular point of $f$.

This happens when singular points accumulate at $z_0$, or when $f$ is singular along a whole curve or region through $z_0$.

Examples:

- $z = 0$ is a non-isolated singularity of $f(z) = 1/\sin(1/z)$. The singular points $z = 1/(n\pi)$, $n \in \mathbb{Z} \setminus \{0\}$, accumulate at $0$, so no punctured disc around $0$ is free of them.
- $z = 0$ is a non-isolated singularity of $f(z) = \tan(1/z)$, with the singular points $z = 2/((2n+1)\pi)$ accumulating at $0$.
- $z = 0$ is a non-isolated singularity of $f(z) = 1/(e^{1/z}-1)$, with the singular points $z = 1/(2\pi i n)$ accumulating at $0$.
- Every point of the non-positive real axis is a non-isolated singularity of the principal logarithm $\operatorname{Log} z$, since $\operatorname{Log}$ is discontinuous at every point of that ray.

<Note>

$f(z) = |z|^2$ has no singular points. It is not analytic at any point in any neighborhood of any point, so no point satisfies the definition of a singular point.

</Note>

## Worked Example

Determine where $f(z) = |z|^2$ is analytic. Use the Cauchy-Riemann equations.

<Solution>

Write $z=x+iy$. Then

$$
f(z) = x^2+y^2 + i0
$$

so $u(x,y)=x^2+y^2$ and $v(x,y)=0$. The Cauchy-Riemann equations require

$$
u_x=v_y, \qquad u_y=-v_x
$$

Here,

$$
u_x=2x, \qquad u_y=2y, \qquad v_x=v_y=0
$$

Both equations hold only when $x=y=0$. Since the partial derivatives are continuous, $f$ is complex differentiable at $0$.

The equations do not hold on any neighborhood of $0$. Therefore $f$ is analytic nowhere.

</Solution>
