The residue of $f$ at an isolated singularity $\alpha$ is the coefficient $a_{-1}$ of $(z-\alpha)^{-1}$ in the [Laurent series](/s5/calculus/complex-functions/laurent-series) of $f$ centered at $\alpha$.

$$
\text{Res}(f, \alpha) = a_{-1}
$$

At a removable singularity, all negative-power coefficients are $0$, so $\text{Res}(f, \alpha) = 0$.

## Cauchy's Residue Theorem

Let $C$ be a simple closed positively oriented [contour](/s5/calculus/complex-functions/contour). Let $f$ be analytic inside and on $C$, except at finitely many isolated singularities $z_1, z_2, \ldots, z_n$ inside $C$. Then

$$
\oint_C f(z)\,\text{d}z = 2\pi i \sum_{k=1}^{n} \text{Res}(f, z_k)
$$

It generalizes [Cauchy's integral formula](/s5/calculus/complex-functions/cauchys-integral-formula).

## Residue at a Pole

Let $\alpha$ be a pole of order $n$ of $f$. Then

$$
\text{Res}(f, \alpha) = \frac{1}{(n-1)!} \lim_{z \to \alpha} \frac{\text{d}^{n-1}}{\text{d}z^{n-1}} \left[(z-\alpha)^n f(z)\right]
$$

For a simple pole,

$$
\text{Res}(f, \alpha) = \lim_{z \to \alpha} (z-\alpha) f(z)
$$

Examples:

- $\text{Res}\left(\exp\left(\frac{2}{z}\right), 0\right) = 2$
- $\text{Res}\left(\frac{3}{2z + z^2 - z^3}, 0\right) = \frac{3}{2}$

## Worked Examples

### Residue from the Laurent Series

Find $\text{Res}\left(\exp\left(\frac{2}{z}\right), 0\right)$.

$$
\exp\left(\frac{2}{z}\right) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{2^n}{n!\, z^n} = 1 + \frac{2}{z} + \frac{2}{z^2} + \cdots
$$

The coefficient of $z^{-1}$ is $2$, so the residue is $2$.

Find $\text{Res}\left(\frac{\pi \cot(\pi z)}{z^2}, 0\right)$.

$$
\pi \cot(\pi z) = \frac{1}{z} - \frac{\pi^2 z}{3} - \cdots
$$

$$
\frac{\pi \cot(\pi z)}{z^2} = \frac{1}{z^3} - \frac{\pi^2}{3z} - \cdots
$$

The residue is $-\frac{\pi^2}{3}$.

### Residue at a Simple Pole

Find $\text{Res}\left(\frac{3}{2z + z^2 - z^3}, 0\right)$.

The denominator is $z(2 + z - z^2)$, so $z = 0$ is a simple pole.

$$
\text{Res} = \lim_{z \to 0} z \cdot \frac{3}{z(2 + z - z^2)} = \frac{3}{2}
$$

### Residue at a Higher Order Pole

Find $\text{Res}\left(\frac{e^z}{(z-2)^3}, 2\right)$.

$z = 2$ is a pole of order 3.

$$
\text{Res} = \frac{1}{2!} \lim_{z \to 2} \frac{\text{d}^2}{\text{d}z^2} e^z = \frac{e^2}{2}
$$

### Removable Singularity

$\frac{\sin z}{z}$ has a removable singularity at $0$, so $\text{Res}\left(\frac{\sin z}{z}, 0\right) = 0$.

### Contour Integral over 2 Poles

Evaluate $\int_C \frac{e^z}{z^2 (z-1)}\,\text{d}z$ where $C$ is $|z| = 2$ with positive orientation.

Both $z = 0$ (order 2) and $z = 1$ (order 1) lie inside $C$.

$$
\text{Res}(f, 1) = \lim_{z \to 1} \frac{e^z}{z^2} = e
$$

$$
\text{Res}(f, 0) = \lim_{z \to 0} \frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}z} \frac{e^z}{z-1} = \lim_{z \to 0} \frac{e^z (z-1) - e^z}{(z-1)^2} = -2
$$

$$
\int_C f(z)\,\text{d}z = 2\pi i (e - 2)
$$

### Contour Away from the Origin

Evaluate $\int_C \frac{\text{d}z}{z^4 + 4}$ where $C$ is $|z-1| = 2$ with positive orientation.

The roots of $z^4 = -4$ are $\pm 1 \pm i$. Their distances from $1$ are $1$, $1$, $\sqrt{5}$, $\sqrt{5}$, so only $1 + i$ and $1 - i$ lie inside $C$.

For a simple pole $z_k$, using $z_k^4 = -4$,

$$
\text{Res}(f, z_k) = \frac{1}{4z_k^3} = \frac{z_k}{4z_k^4} = -\frac{z_k}{16}
$$

$$
\text{Res}(f, 1+i) + \text{Res}(f, 1-i) = -\frac{(1+i) + (1-i)}{16} = -\frac{1}{8}
$$

$$
\int_C f(z)\,\text{d}z = 2\pi i \left(-\frac{1}{8}\right) = -\frac{\pi i}{4}
$$

### Contour Integral over 3 Poles

Evaluate $\int_C \frac{\text{d}z}{z^4 + z^3 - 2z^2}$ where $C$ is $|z| = 3$ with positive orientation.

Factor the denominator.

$$
z^4 + z^3 - 2z^2 = z^2 (z-1)(z+2)
$$

All singularities lie inside $C$. $z = 0$ is a pole of order 2. $z = 1$ and $z = -2$ are simple poles.

$$
\text{Res}(f, 1) = \lim_{z \to 1} \frac{1}{z^2(z+2)} = \frac{1}{3}
$$

$$
\text{Res}(f, -2) = \lim_{z \to -2} \frac{1}{z^2(z-1)} = -\frac{1}{12}
$$

$$
\text{Res}(f, 0) = \lim_{z \to 0} \frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}z} \frac{1}{z^2 + z - 2} = \lim_{z \to 0} \frac{-(2z+1)}{(z^2+z-2)^2} = -\frac{1}{4}
$$

$$
\oint_C f(z)\,\text{d}z = 2\pi i \left(\frac{1}{3} - \frac{1}{12} - \frac{1}{4}\right) = 0
$$
