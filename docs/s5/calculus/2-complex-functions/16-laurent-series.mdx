For $0 \le r < R \le \infty$, the annulus $A(r, R, \alpha)$ is

$$
A(r, R, \alpha) = \{z \in \mathbb{C} : r < |z-\alpha| < R\}
$$

Let $c_n \in \mathbb{C}$ for each $n \in \mathbb{Z}$. The doubly infinite series

$$
\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty} c_n (z-\alpha)^n = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} c_{-n}(z-\alpha)^{-n} + \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} c_n (z-\alpha)^n
$$

is called a Laurent series, provided both series on the right-hand side converge.

## Laurent's Theorem

Let $f$ be analytic in the annulus $A(r, R, \alpha)$. Then $f$ has a Laurent series representation on $A(r, R, \alpha)$.

$$
f(z) = \sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty} c_n (z-\alpha)^n, \quad z \in A(r, R, \alpha)
$$

The coefficients are

<Formula>

$$
c_n = \frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{|x-\alpha|=s} \frac{f(x)}{(x-\alpha)^{n+1}}\,\text{d}x
$$

<Symbols>
  <Sym s="n">an integer</Sym>
  <Sym s="s">any radius with $r < s < R$</Sym>
</Symbols>

</Formula>

The circle $|\zeta-\alpha| = s$ is oriented counterclockwise.

## Properties

- The Laurent series representation of $f$ on $A(r, R, \alpha)$ is unique.
- A Laurent series can be differentiated term by term inside its annulus of convergence.

<Note>

A function analytic in $D_R(\alpha)$ has $c_n = 0$ for all $n < 0$, so its Laurent series is its [Taylor series](/s5/calculus/complex-functions/taylor-series).

</Note>

Examples:

- $\frac{\cos z - 1}{z^4} = -\frac{1}{2z^2} + \frac{1}{24} - \frac{z^2}{720} + \cdots$ centered at $0$
- $\exp\left(-\frac{1}{z^2}\right) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^n}{n!\, z^{2n}}$ centered at $0$

## Finding a Laurent Series

To find a Laurent series of $f$ centered at $\alpha$:

1. Identify the singular points of $f$.
2. Calculate the distance from each singular point to $\alpha$.
3. Use these distances to split the plane into annuli centered at $\alpha$.
4. Choose the annulus where the series is required.
5. Rewrite $f$ into terms that can be expanded around $\alpha$.
   Partial fractions, algebraic division, and substitutions may be required.
6. Expand each term using a Taylor series or a geometric series.
7. Choose the form of each geometric series that converges on the selected annulus.

$$
\frac{1}{1-w} = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} w^n, \quad |w| < 1
$$

$$
\frac{1}{1-w} = -\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} w^{-n-1}, \quad |w| > 1
$$

8. Combine the resulting series.
9. State the annulus of convergence.
10. Check that every term converges on the stated annulus.

The singular points determine the possible annuli. The selected annulus determines whether each factor is expanded in positive or negative powers of $z-\alpha$.

## Different Annuli

The same function has a different Laurent series on each annulus bounded by its singular points.

Consider

$$
f(z) = \frac{3}{2+z-z^2} = \frac{1}{2-z} + \frac{1}{1+z}
$$

The singular points are $z = 2$ and $z = -1$ with $|z| = 2$ and $|z| = 1$. The centered series at $0$ are as follows.

- $|z| < 1$  
  $\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \left(\frac{1}{2^{n+1}} + (-1)^n\right) z^n$
- $1 < |z| < 2$  
  $\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{z^n}{2^{n+1}} + \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} (-1)^n z^{-n-1}$
- $|z| > 2$  
  $\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \left((-1)^n - 2^n\right) z^{-n-1}$

## Worked Examples

### Coefficient Formula

Find $c_{-2}$ of $f(z) = \frac{1}{z^2}$ centered at $0$.

<Solution>

Since $f(z) = z^{-2}$ already consists of one Laurent term, its only nonzero coefficient is the coefficient of $(z-0)^{-2}$.

$$
c_{-2} = \frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{|x|=s} \frac{x^{-2}}{x^{-2+1}}\,\text{d}x = \frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{|x|=s} x^{-1}\,\text{d}x = \frac{1}{2\pi i} \cdot 2\pi i = 1
$$

For $n \neq -2$, the integrand is $\zeta^{-n-3}$. Its integral is $0$ because

$$
\int_{|x|=s} x^k\,\text{d}x = 0, \quad k \neq -1
$$

</Solution>

### Cosine over a Power

Find the Laurent series of $\frac{\cos z - 1}{z^4}$ centered at $0$.

<Solution>

Expand the numerator at $0$.

$$
\cos z - 1 = -\frac{z^2}{2} + \frac{z^4}{24} - \frac{z^6}{720} + \cdots
$$

Divide each term by $z^4$.

$$
\frac{\cos z - 1}{z^4} = -\frac{1}{2z^2} + \frac{1}{24} - \frac{z^2}{720} + \cdots
$$

The singular point is $z=0$. The series is valid on $0 < |z| < \infty$.

</Solution>

### Exponential of a Reciprocal

Find the Laurent series of $\exp\left(-\frac{1}{z^2}\right)$ centered at $0$.

<Solution>

Use the Taylor series $e^w = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{w^n}{n!}$ and substitute $w = -\frac{1}{z^2}$.

$$
\exp\left(-\frac{1}{z^2}\right) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^n}{n!\, z^{2n}} = 1 - \frac{1}{z^2} + \frac{1}{2z^4} - \cdots
$$

The singular point is $z=0$. The exponential series converges for every finite value of $w$, so the Laurent series is valid on $0 < |z| < \infty$.

</Solution>

### Simple Fractions

Find the Laurent series of $\frac{1}{z(1-z)}$ centered at $0$ in $0 < |z| < 1$.

<Solution>

Separate the factor $z$ and expand the remaining factor geometrically.

$$
\frac{1}{z(1-z)} = \frac{1}{z} + \frac{1}{1-z} = \frac{1}{z} + \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} z^n
$$

The geometric series requires $|z|<1$. Therefore, the Laurent series is valid on $0<|z|<1$.

</Solution>

Find the Laurent series of $\frac{1}{(z-1)(z-3)}$ centered at $0$ in $1 < |z| < 3$.

<Solution>

Use partial fractions.

$$
\frac{1}{(z-1)(z-3)} = \frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{1}{z-3} - \frac{1}{z-1}\right)
$$

For $|z| < 3$,

$$
\frac{1}{z-3} = -\frac{1}{3} \cdot \frac{1}{1 - \frac{z}{3}} = -\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{z^n}{3^{n+1}}
$$

For $|z| > 1$,

$$
\frac{1}{z-1} = \frac{1}{z} \cdot \frac{1}{1 - \frac{1}{z}} = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} z^{-n-1}
$$

$$
\frac{1}{(z-1)(z-3)} = -\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{z^n}{3^{n+1}} - \frac{1}{2}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} z^{-n-1}
$$

The first series requires $|z|<3$. The second requires $|z|>1$. Both converge on $1<|z|<3$, which is the required annulus.

</Solution>

### 3 Series for 1 Function

Find all Laurent series of $f(z) = \frac{3}{2+z-z^2}$ centered at $0$.

<Solution>

Factor $2 + z - z^2 = (2-z)(1+z)$. Partial fractions give

$$
f(z) = \frac{1}{2-z} + \frac{1}{1+z}
$$

The singular points $z = -1$ and $z = 2$ split the plane into $|z| < 1$, $1 < |z| < 2$, and $|z| > 2$.

For $|z| < 2$,

$$
\frac{1}{2-z} = \frac{1}{2} \cdot \frac{1}{1 - \frac{z}{2}} = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{z^n}{2^{n+1}}
$$

For $|z| > 2$,

$$
\frac{1}{2-z} = -\frac{1}{z} \cdot \frac{1}{1 - \frac{2}{z}} = -\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} 2^n z^{-n-1}
$$

For $|z| < 1$,

$$
\frac{1}{1+z} = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} (-1)^n z^n
$$

For $|z| > 1$,

$$
\frac{1}{1+z} = \frac{1}{z} \cdot \frac{1}{1 + \frac{1}{z}} = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} (-1)^n z^{-n-1}
$$

Combine the matching pieces.

- For $|z| < 1$, the series is $\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \left(\frac{1}{2^{n+1}} + (-1)^n\right) z^n$.
- For $1 < |z| < 2$, the series is $\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{z^n}{2^{n+1}} + \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} (-1)^n z^{-n-1}$.
- For $|z| > 2$, the series is $\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \left((-1)^n - 2^n\right) z^{-n-1}$.

</Solution>
