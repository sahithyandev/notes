Let $D$ be a domain in the $xy$-plane. A real-valued function $u(x,y)$ is harmonic in $D$ **iff**:

- $u$ has continuous second-order partial derivatives in $D$.
- $u$ satisfies Laplace's equation throughout $D$:

$$
\nabla^2 u = u_{xx} + u_{yy} = 0
$$

## Component Functions

**If** $f(z) = u(x,y) + iv(x,y)$ is analytic in a domain $D$, **then** $u$ and $v$ are harmonic in $D$.

<Note title="Proof Hint">

- Start with the [Cauchy-Riemann equations](/s5/calculus/complex-functions/derivatives#cauchy-riemann-equations) $u_x = v_y$, $u_y = -v_x$
- Differentiate $u_x = v_y$ with respect to $x$
- Differentiate $u_y = -v_x$ with respect to $y$
- Add the results and use equality of mixed partials of $v$

</Note>

## Harmonic Conjugate

Let $u$ and $v$ be real-valued functions on a region $D \subseteq \mathbb{R}^2$. $v$ is a harmonic conjugate of $u$ **iff** $f(z) = u(x,y) + iv(x,y)$ is analytic on $D$.

A harmonic conjugate of $u$ exists only **if** $u$ is harmonic on $D$. A harmonic $u$ does not always have one.

If $D$ is [simply connected](/s5/calculus/vector-valued-functions/connectedness#simply-connected-region), a harmonic conjugate always exists and is unique up to an additive constant. Otherwise it may fail to exist.

For example: $u = \ln|z|$ is harmonic on $\mathbb{C} \setminus \{0\}$, but the only candidate $v = \arg z$ is multivalued, so no harmonic conjugate exists there.

<Note>

Order matters. $u$ and $v$ can both be harmonic on $D$ without $v$ being a harmonic conjugate of $u$.

</Note>

### Finding a Conjugate

Given a harmonic $u(x,y)$ on a simply connected domain:

1. Check that $u_{xx} + u_{yy} = 0$.
2. Compute $u_x$ and $u_y$.
3. Use the Cauchy-Riemann equation $v_y = u_x$. Integrate with respect to $y$ to get $v = \int u_x \, dy + \phi(x)$, where $\phi(x)$ is an unknown function of $x$ alone.
4. Differentiate this $v$ with respect to $x$ and equate it to $-u_y$, from the Cauchy-Riemann equation $v_x = -u_y$. All terms containing $y$ cancel because $u$ is harmonic, which leaves an expression for $\phi'(x)$.
5. Integrate $\phi'(x)$ to get $\phi(x)$, including an arbitrary constant $C$.
6. Substitute $\phi(x)$ into $v$. Then $f = u + iv$ is analytic, and can be written in terms of $z$.

Integrating $v_x = -u_y$ with respect to $x$ first and adding an unknown function of $y$ works the same way.

## Worked Example

Find a harmonic conjugate $v$ of $u(x,y) = y^3 - 3x^2y$, and the corresponding analytic function $f$.

$$
u_x = -6xy, \qquad u_y = 3y^2 - 3x^2
$$

Cauchy-Riemann $v_y = u_x$:

$$
v_y = -6xy \implies v = -3xy^2 + \phi(x)
$$

Cauchy-Riemann $v_x = -u_y$:

$$
-3y^2 + \phi'(x) = -3y^2 + 3x^2 \implies \phi'(x) = 3x^2
$$

$$
\phi(x) = x^3 + C
$$

$$
v(x,y) = x^3 - 3xy^2 + C
$$

$$
f(z) = (y^3 - 3x^2y) + i(x^3 - 3xy^2 + C) = iz^3 + iC
$$
