Let $S \subseteq \mathbb{C}$, let $f: S \to \mathbb{C}$ be a [function](/s5/calculus/complex-functions/introduction), and let $z_0$ be a [limit point](/s5/calculus/complex-functions/introduction#limit-point) of $S$, so every deleted neighborhood of $z_0$ contains at least 1 point of $S$. $z_0$ need not belong to $S$. For $\omega_0 \in \mathbb{C}$,

$$
\lim_{z \to z_0} f(z) = \omega_0
$$

**iff**, for each $\epsilon > 0$, there exists $\delta > 0$ such that, for each $z \in S$,

$$
0 < |z-z_0| < \delta \implies |f(z)-\omega_0| < \epsilon
$$

$f(z)$ can be made arbitrarily close to $\omega_0$ by choosing $z$ sufficiently close to, but distinct from, $z_0$.

<Note>

$z_0$ need not lie in the domain of $f$. It only needs to be a limit point of the domain.

</Note>

## Uniqueness of Limits

If a limit of $f: S \to \mathbb{C}$ exists at a limit point of $S$, the limit is unique.

## Limits via Real and Imaginary Parts

Let $f(z) = u(x,y)+iv(x,y)$, $z_0 = x_0+iy_0$, $\omega_0 = u_0+iv_0$. Then

$$
\lim_{z \to z_0} f(z) = \omega_0
$$

**iff**

$$
\lim_{(x,y) \to (x_0,y_0)} u(x,y) = u_0 \quad \text{and} \quad \lim_{(x,y) \to (x_0,y_0)} v(x,y) = v_0
$$

## Limit Laws

Suppose $\displaystyle\lim_{z \to z_0} f(z) = \omega_0$ and $\displaystyle\lim_{z \to z_0} g(z) = \Omega_0$ for the rest of this section.

### Sum Law

$$
\displaystyle\lim_{z \to z_0} (f(z)+g(z)) = \omega_0+\Omega_0
$$

<Note title="Proof Hint">

- Fix $\epsilon > 0$
- Choose $\delta_1$ such that $0 < |z-z_0| < \delta_1 \implies |f(z)-\omega_0| < \epsilon/2$
- Choose $\delta_2$ such that $0 < |z-z_0| < \delta_2 \implies |g(z)-\Omega_0| < \epsilon/2$
- Let $\delta = \min(\delta_1, \delta_2)$
- Rewrite $|f(z)+g(z)-(\omega_0+\Omega_0)|$ as $|(f(z)-\omega_0)+(g(z)-\Omega_0)|$
- Apply the triangle inequality

</Note>

### Product Law

$$
\displaystyle\lim_{z \to z_0} (f(z)g(z)) = \omega_0\Omega_0
$$

<Note title="Proof Hint">

- Rewrite $f(z)g(z)-\omega_0\Omega_0$ as $(f(z)-\omega_0)g(z)+\omega_0(g(z)-\Omega_0)$
- Apply the definition of the limit of $g$ with $\epsilon = 1$ to get $|g(z)| < |\Omega_0|+1$ near $z_0$
- Choose $\delta_1$ such that $|f(z)-\omega_0| < \dfrac{\epsilon}{2(|\Omega_0|+1)}$
- Choose $\delta_2$ such that $|g(z)-\Omega_0| < \dfrac{\epsilon}{2(|\omega_0|+1)}$
- Let $\delta$ be the minimum of all 3 radii
- Apply the triangle inequality

</Note>

### Quotient Law

$$
\displaystyle\lim_{z \to z_0} \frac{f(z)}{g(z)} = \frac{\omega_0}{\Omega_0}, \quad \text{provided} \quad \Omega_0 \ne 0
$$

<Note title="Proof Hint">

- Prove $\displaystyle\lim_{z \to z_0} \frac{1}{g(z)} = \frac{1}{\Omega_0}$, then apply the product law with $f(z)$
- Apply the definition of the limit of $g$ with $\epsilon = |\Omega_0|/2$ to get $|g(z)| > |\Omega_0|/2$ near $z_0$
- Rewrite $\left|\dfrac{1}{g(z)}-\dfrac{1}{\Omega_0}\right|$ as $\dfrac{|g(z)-\Omega_0|}{|g(z)||\Omega_0|}$
- Bound it by $\dfrac{2|g(z)-\Omega_0|}{|\Omega_0|^2}$
- Choose $\delta$ such that $|g(z)-\Omega_0| < \dfrac{\epsilon|\Omega_0|^2}{2}$

</Note>

## Limit of a Composition

Let $f: B \to \mathbb{C}$ and $g: A \to B$ be functions. Suppose

$$
\lim_{\omega \to \omega_0} g(\omega) = z_0 \quad \text{and} \quad \lim_{z \to z_0} f(z) = L
$$

and $g(\omega) \ne z_0$ for all $\omega$ sufficiently close to $\omega_0$, $\omega \ne \omega_0$. Then

$$
\lim_{\omega \to \omega_0} f(g(\omega)) = L
$$

<Note title="Proof Hint">

- Fix $\epsilon > 0$
- Choose $\delta_1$ such that $0 < |z-z_0| < \delta_1 \implies |f(z)-L| < \epsilon$
- Choose $\delta_2$ such that $0 < |\omega-\omega_0| < \delta_2 \implies |g(\omega)-z_0| < \delta_1$
- Choose $\delta_3$ such that $0 < |\omega-\omega_0| < \delta_3 \implies g(\omega) \ne z_0$
- Let $\delta = \min(\delta_2, \delta_3)$
- Conclude $0 < |g(\omega)-z_0| < \delta_1$, so $|f(g(\omega))-L| < \epsilon$

</Note>

## Limits Involving Infinity

Let $z_0, \omega_0 \in \mathbb{C}$.

### Infinite Limit at a Point

$$
\lim_{z \to z_0} f(z) = \infty \iff \lim_{z \to z_0} \frac{1}{f(z)} = 0
$$

where $f(z) \ne 0$ for all $z$ sufficiently close to $z_0$ with $z \ne z_0$.

<Note title="Proof Hint">

- Start with the definition $\displaystyle\lim_{z \to z_0} f(z) = \infty$ **iff** $\forall M > 0, \exists \delta > 0$ such that $0 < |z-z_0| < \delta \implies |f(z)| > M$
- Set $M = 1/\epsilon$
- Rewrite $|f(z)| > 1/\epsilon$ as $\left|\dfrac{1}{f(z)}-0\right| < \epsilon$
- Reverse the steps for the other direction

</Note>

### Finite Limit at Infinity

$$
\lim_{z \to \infty} f(z) = \omega_0 \iff \lim_{z \to 0} f\left(\frac{1}{z}\right) = \omega_0
$$

<Note title="Proof Hint">

- Start with the definition $\displaystyle\lim_{z \to \infty} f(z) = \omega_0$ **iff** $\forall \epsilon > 0, \exists R > 0$ such that $|z| > R \implies |f(z)-\omega_0| < \epsilon$
- Set $\delta = 1/R$
- Substitute $z \mapsto 1/z$ so $0 < |z| < \delta$ becomes $|1/z| > R$
- Reverse the steps for the other direction

</Note>

### Infinite Limit at Infinity

$\displaystyle\lim_{z \to \infty} f(z) = \infty$ **iff** $\forall M > 0, \exists R > 0$ such that $|z| > R \implies |f(z)| > M$.

$$
\lim_{z \to \infty} f(z) = \infty \iff \lim_{z \to 0} \frac{1}{f(1/z)} = 0
$$

<Note title="Proof Hint">

- Set $M = 1/\epsilon$ and $\delta = 1/R$
- Substitute $z \mapsto 1/z$
- Rewrite $|f(1/z)| > 1/\epsilon$ as $\left|\dfrac{1}{f(1/z)}\right| < \epsilon$
- Reverse the steps for the other direction

</Note>
