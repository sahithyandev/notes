## Continuity

Let $D \subseteq \mathbb{C}$ and let $f: D \to \mathbb{C}$ be a function.

### Continuity at a Limit Point

<Theorem>

Let $f: D \to \mathbb{C}$, $z_0 \in D$.

- If $z_0$ is not an [isolated point](/s5/calculus/complex-functions/introduction#isolated-point) of $D$, $f$ is continuous at $z_0$ **iff** $\displaystyle\lim_{z \to z_0} f(z) = f(z_0)$
- If $z_0$ is an isolated point of $D$, $f$ is continuous at $z_0$

<Proof label="Proof Hint">

For a non-isolated point, write the definition of continuity at $z_0$ using
$|f(z)-f(z_0)|$ and compare it with the definition of the limit as
$z \to z_0$.

For an isolated point, choose a radius $r>0$ such that
$D \cap B(z_0,r)=\{z_0\}$. There are no other points of $D$ sufficiently close
to $z_0$, so the epsilon-delta condition is vacuously satisfied.

</Proof>
    
</Theorem>

### Nonvanishing Near a Point

<Theorem>

If $f$ is continuous and non-zero at $z_0$, then $f(z) \ne 0$ throughout some neighborhood of $z_0$.

</Theorem>

<Proof label="Proof Hint">

Set $\varepsilon=\dfrac{|f(z_0)|}{2}>0$. Continuity gives a neighborhood of
$z_0$ in which

$$
|f(z)-f(z_0)|<\frac{|f(z_0)|}{2}
$$

Use the reverse triangle inequality to show that $|f(z)|$ is positive in this
neighborhood.

</Proof>

### Boundedness on a Closed and Bounded Set

<Theorem>

If $f$ is continuous on a closed and bounded set $R \subseteq \mathbb{C}$, there exists a non-negative real number $M$ such that

$$
|f(z)| \le M
$$

for all $z \in R$, with equality for at least 1 point in $R$.

</Theorem>

<Proof label="Proof Hint">

Use that a closed and bounded subset of $\mathbb{C}\cong\mathbb{R}^2$ is
compact. $|f(z)|$ is continuous on $R$, so the [Extreme
Value Theorem](/s1/mathematics/real-analysis/continuity-theorems#extreme-value-theorem) gives a point $z_0\in R$ where it attains its maximum. Set
$M=|f(z_0)|$.

</Proof>

## Derivative

### Differentiation Formulas

Let $c \in \mathbb{C}$, and let $f$, $g$ be functions differentiable at $z \in \mathbb{C}$. Then:

- $\dfrac{\text{d}}{\text{d}z}\,c = 0$
- $\dfrac{\text{d}}{\text{d}z}\,(cf(z)) = cf'(z)$
- $\dfrac{\text{d}}{\text{d}z}\,z^n = nz^{n-1}$, for positive integer $n$
- $\dfrac{\text{d}}{\text{d}z}\,(f(z)+g(z)) = f'(z)+g'(z)$
- $\dfrac{\text{d}}{\text{d}z}\,(f(z)g(z)) = f(z)g'(z)+f'(z)g(z)$
- $\dfrac{\text{d}}{\text{d}z}\,\dfrac{f(z)}{g(z)} = \dfrac{g(z)f'(z)-f(z)g'(z)}{(g(z))^2}$, provided $g(z) \ne 0$

## Polar Cauchy-Riemann Equations

### Coordinate Change

Let $z_0=r_0e^{i\theta_0}$ with $r_0>0$. Write

$$
f(z)=u(r,\theta)+iv(r,\theta), \qquad x=r\cos\theta, \qquad y=r\sin\theta
$$

The polar form is valid only where $r>0$. The chain rule gives

$$
\begin{pmatrix}u_x\\u_y\end{pmatrix}
=
\begin{pmatrix}
\cos\theta&-\dfrac{\sin\theta}{r}\\
\sin\theta&\dfrac{\cos\theta}{r}
\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}u_r\\u_\theta\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
\begin{pmatrix}v_x\\v_y\end{pmatrix}
=
\begin{pmatrix}
\cos\theta&-\dfrac{\sin\theta}{r}\\
\sin\theta&\dfrac{\cos\theta}{r}
\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}v_r\\v_\theta\end{pmatrix}
$$

The factors $1/r$ occur because changing $\theta$ by $\text{d}\theta$ moves a distance $r\,\text{d}\theta$ in the plane.

### Polar Equations

Substituting into the Cartesian equations $u_x=v_y$ and $u_y=-v_x$ gives

$$
u_r=\frac{1}{r}v_\theta, \qquad v_r=-\frac{1}{r}u_\theta
$$

Equivalently,

$$
ru_r=v_\theta, \qquad u_\theta=-rv_r
$$

### Change of Variables in an Integral

For a polar substitution, use the map

$$
T(r,\theta)=(x,y)=(r\cos\theta,r\sin\theta)
$$

The input is $(r,\theta)$. The output is $(x,y)$. The relevant Jacobian is therefore

$$
\frac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(r,\theta)}
=
\begin{pmatrix}
\dfrac{\partial x}{\partial r}&\dfrac{\partial x}{\partial\theta}\\[4pt]
\dfrac{\partial y}{\partial r}&\dfrac{\partial y}{\partial\theta}
\end{pmatrix}
=
\begin{pmatrix}
\cos\theta&-r\sin\theta\\
\sin\theta&r\cos\theta
\end{pmatrix}
$$

Its determinant is $r$. Therefore,

$$
\text{d}x\,\text{d}y=r\,\text{d}r\,\text{d}\theta
$$

The integral transforms as

$$
\iint_R f(x,y)\,\text{d}x\,\text{d}y
=
\iint_S f(r\cos\theta,r\sin\theta)\,r\,\text{d}r\,\text{d}\theta
$$

The inverse Jacobian $\partial(r,\theta)/\partial(x,y)$ is used when recovering $r$ and $\theta$ from $(x,y)$. It is not the Jacobian used for the area substitution above.

## Worked Examples

### Continuity Example

Determine whether $f(z) = \dfrac{z^2+1}{z-i}$ is continuous at $z_0 = 1$.

<Solution>

The numerator and denominator are polynomials. The denominator at $z_0$ is

$$
1-i \ne 0
$$

Therefore the quotient is defined and continuous at $1$. Its value is

$$
f(1) = \frac{1+1}{1-i} = \frac{2(1+i)}{2} = 1+i
$$

</Solution>

### Derivative Example

Find the derivative of $f(z) = \dfrac{z^2+1}{z-i}$ at $z = 1$.

<Solution>

Use the quotient rule with $p(z) = z^2+1$ and $q(z) = z-i$:

$$
f'(z) = \frac{q(z)p'(z)-p(z)q'(z)}{q(z)^2}
= \frac{(z-i)2z-(z^2+1)}{(z-i)^2}
$$

At $z=1$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
f'(1)
&= \frac{2(1-i)-2}{(1-i)^2} \\
&= \frac{-2i}{-2i} \\
&= 1
\end{aligned}
$$

</Solution>
