If $f$ is analytic on an open set $G$, $f'$ is continuous on $G$.

Let $u$ and $v$ be real-valued functions with continuous first-order partial derivatives on an open set $U \subseteq \mathbb{C}$, and define $f(z) = u(x,y)+iv(x,y)$, $z = x+iy$. Then $f$ is analytic in $U$ **iff** $u$ and $v$ satisfy the [Cauchy-Riemann equations](/s1/mathematics/complex-analysis/cauchy-riemann-equations) throughout $U$.

## Properties

Let $f$ be analytic on a domain $D$.

- $f'$ is analytic on $D$. So $f$ is infinitely differentiable on $D$.
- $\operatorname{Re} f$ and $\operatorname{Im} f$ are harmonic on $D$.
- If $f'(z) = 0$ throughout $D$, then $f$ is constant on $D$.
- If $|f|$ is constant on $D$, then $f$ is constant on $D$.
- If $\operatorname{Re} f$ is constant on $D$, or $\operatorname{Im} f$ is constant on $D$, then $f$ is constant on $D$.
- If $f$ is not constant on $D$, its zeros are isolated.

<Proof label="Proof Hint">

For $|f|$ constant:

- If $|f| = 0$, then $f = 0$
- Otherwise write $|f|^2 = u^2+v^2 = c$ with $c > 0$
- Differentiate in $x$ and $y$, apply the Cauchy-Riemann equations
- Get $u_x = u_y = v_x = v_y = 0$, so $f' = 0$

</Proof>

Let $f$ and $g$ be analytic in a domain $D$.

- $f+g$, $f-g$, and $fg$ are analytic in $D$.
- $f/g$ is analytic in $D$ provided $g$ does not vanish at any point of $D$.
- If $f$ is analytic in $D$ and $g$ is analytic on a domain containing the image of $D$ under $f$, then $g \circ f$ is analytic in $D$, and

$$
\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}z}\,g(f(z)) = g'(f(z))\,f'(z)
$$

## Singular Point

<Aka>Singularity</Aka>. A singular point of a function $f$ is a point where $f$
is not analytic.

### Isolated Singularity

$z_0$ is an isolated singularity **iff** a small enough disc around $z_0$ contains no singular point other than $z_0$.

### Non-Isolated Singularity

$z_0$ is a non-isolated singularity **iff** every disc around $z_0$ contains another singular point.

## Worked Example

Determine where $f(z) = |z|^2$ is analytic. Use the Cauchy-Riemann equations.

<Solution>

Write $z=x+iy$. Then

$$
f(z) = x^2+y^2 + i0
$$

so $u(x,y)=x^2+y^2$ and $v(x,y)=0$. The Cauchy-Riemann equations require

$$
u_x=v_y, \qquad u_y=-v_x
$$

Here,

$$
u_x=2x, \qquad u_y=2y, \qquad v_x=v_y=0
$$

Both equations hold only when $x=y=0$. Since the partial derivatives are continuous, $f$ is complex differentiable at $0$.

The equations do not hold on any neighborhood of $0$. Therefore $f$ is analytic nowhere.

</Solution>

Classify $z=0$ for $f(z)=1/z$.

<Solution>

$f$ is not defined at $z=0$, so it is not analytic there. Therefore, $0$ is a singular point.

For every $z\neq 0$, $f$ is analytic. There are no other singular points. Therefore, a small enough disc around $0$ contains no singular point other than $0$.

Hence, $0$ is an isolated singularity.

</Solution>

Classify $z=0$ for $f(z)=z^2$.

<Solution>

$f$ is a polynomial, so it is analytic at $0$. Therefore, $0$ is not a singular point.

</Solution>

Classify $z=0$ for $f$.

$$
f(z)=\frac{1}{\sin \left(\frac{1}{z} \right)}
$$

<Solution>

$f$ is not defined at $z=0$. Therefore, $0$ is a singular point.

The other singular points occur when

$$
\sin(1/z)=0
$$

so

$$
\frac{1}{z}=n\pi
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
z=\frac{1}{n\pi}, \quad n\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
$$

These singular points approach $0$. Every disc around $0$ contains another singular point.

Hence, $0$ is a non-isolated singularity.

</Solution>
