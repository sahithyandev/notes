Let $f$ be a function whose domain contains a neighborhood $|z-z_0| < \epsilon$ of $z_0$. The derivative of $f$ at $z_0$ is

$$
f'(z_0) = \lim_{z \to z_0} \frac{f(z)-f(z_0)}{z-z_0}
$$

provided this limit exists. $f$ is differentiable at $z_0$ when $f'(z_0)$ exists.

If $f$ is differentiable at $z_0$, then $f$ is [continuous](/s5/calculus/complex-functions/continuity) at $z_0$.

## Differentiation Formulas

Let $c \in \mathbb{C}$, and let $f$, $g$ be functions differentiable at $z \in \mathbb{C}$. Then:

- $\dfrac{\text{d}}{\text{d}z}\,c = 0$
- $\dfrac{\text{d}}{\text{d}z}\,(cf(z)) = cf'(z)$
- $\dfrac{\text{d}}{\text{d}z}\,z^n = nz^{n-1}$, for positive integer $n$
- $\dfrac{\text{d}}{\text{d}z}\,(f(z)+g(z)) = f'(z)+g'(z)$
- $\dfrac{\text{d}}{\text{d}z}\,(f(z)g(z)) = f(z)g'(z)+f'(z)g(z)$
- $\dfrac{\text{d}}{\text{d}z}\,\dfrac{f(z)}{g(z)} = \dfrac{g(z)f'(z)-f(z)g'(z)}{(g(z))^2}$, provided $g(z) \ne 0$

## Cauchy-Riemann Equations

Let $f(z) = u(x,y)+iv(x,y)$, and suppose $f'(z_0)$ exists at $z_0 = x_0+iy_0$. Then the first-order partial derivatives of $u$ and $v$ exist at $(x_0,y_0)$ and satisfy

$$
u_x = v_y, \qquad u_y = -v_x
$$

at $(x_0,y_0)$, and

$$
f'(z_0) = u_x(x_0,y_0)+iv_x(x_0,y_0)
$$

The equations follow from forcing the derivative limit to agree along the real and imaginary directions of approach.

<Note title="Proof Hint">

- Take $\Delta z = \Delta x$ in the limit, get $f'(z_0) = u_x + iv_x$
- Take $\Delta z = i\,\Delta y$ in the limit, get $f'(z_0) = v_y - iu_y$
- Equate real parts and imaginary parts

</Note>

The equations are necessary but not sufficient. They can hold at $z_0$ while $f'(z_0)$ does not exist. See [Sufficient Conditions for Differentiability](#sufficient-conditions-for-differentiability).

### Polar Form

Suppose $f(z) = u(r,\theta)+iv(r,\theta)$ be defined throughout a neighborhood of a non-zero point $z_0 = r_0e^{i\theta_0}$.

The polar form of the Cauchy-Riemann equations is

$$
ru_r = v_\theta, \qquad u_\theta = -rv_r
$$

at $(r_0,\theta_0)$.

### Jacobian

The Jacobian of $(x,y) \mapsto (u,v)$ is then

$$
\begin{pmatrix} u_x & -v_x \\ v_x & u_x \end{pmatrix}
$$

which acts as multiplication by $u_x + iv_x$. A complex-differentiable map is locally a rotation plus a uniform scaling.

Examples:

- $f(z) = z^2$: $u_x = v_y = 2x$, $u_y = -v_x = -2y$, and $f'(z) = 2x + i2y = 2z$.
- $f(z) = \bar{z}$: $u_x = 1$, $v_y = -1$, so the equations fail everywhere and $\bar{z}$ is nowhere differentiable.

## Sufficient Conditions for Differentiability

Let $f(z) = u(x,y)+iv(x,y)$ be defined throughout a neighborhood $U$ of $z_0 = x_0+iy_0$.

$f'(z_0)$ exists **iff**:

- The first-order partial derivatives of $u$ and $v$ with respect to $x$ and $y$ exist ($u_x$, $u_y$, $v_x$, and $v_y$) everywhere in $U$.
- The partial derivatives are continuous at $(x_0,y_0)$.
- The partial derivatives satisfy the [Cauchy-Riemann equations](#cauchy-riemann-equations) at $(x_0,y_0)$.

When these hold,

$$
f'(z_0) = u_x(x_0,y_0)+iv_x(x_0,y_0)
$$

Or in polar form,

$$
f'(z_0) = e^{-i\theta_0}(u_r(r_0,\theta_0)+iv_r(r_0,\theta_0))
$$
