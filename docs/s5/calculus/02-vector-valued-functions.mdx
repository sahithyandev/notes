## Parametric curves

A particle moving along a curve $C$ in three-dimensional space has $x$, $y$, $z$ coordinates as functions of time $t$:

$$
x = f(t), \qquad y = g(t), \qquad z = h(t)
$$

These are the parametric equations of motion of the particle.

- $t$: the parameter
- $C$: the trajectory of the particle

Restricting $t$ to an interval $[a, b]$ gives

$$
x = f(t), \qquad y = g(t), \qquad z = h(t), \qquad a \le t \le b
$$

The direction in which $C$ is traced as $t$ increases is the orientation of the curve.

Examples:

- The circular helix $x = a\cos t$, $y = a\sin t$, $z = bt$, $0 \le t \le 2\pi$, for fixed real $a > 0$ and $b$.

## Vector-valued functions

A vector-valued function is a function whose domain is a set of real numbers and whose range is a set of vectors.

For component functions $x(t)$, $y(t)$, $z(t)$,

$$
\vec{r}(t) = \langle x(t), y(t), z(t) \rangle = x(t)\hat{i} + y(t)\hat{j} + z(t)\hat{k}
$$

The domain of $\vec{r}$ is the set of all $t$ for which $\vec{r}(t)$ is defined.

As $t$ varies, $\vec{r}(t)$ is positioned with its initial point at the origin, and its terminal point traces out $C$. In this case $\vec{r}(t)$ is the radius vector, or position vector, for $C$.

## Space curves

A vector-valued function $\vec{r}(t)$ whose values are three-dimensional vectors traces out a space curve in three-dimensional space.

Examples:

- $\vec{r}(t) = \langle \cos t, \sin t, 0 \rangle$ traces out the unit circle in the $xy$-plane centered at the origin.
- $\vec{r}(t) = \langle t, t^2, t^3 \rangle$ traces out the twisted cubic.

## Limits and continuity

Let $\vec{r}(t)$ be defined for each $t$ in some open interval containing $a$, except possibly at $a$, and let $L$ be a vector. Then

$$
\lim_{t \to a} \vec{r}(t) = L \quad \text{if} \quad \lim_{t \to a} \lVert \vec{r}(t) - L \rVert = 0
$$

If $\vec{r}(t) = \langle x(t), y(t), z(t) \rangle$ and the limits of the component functions exist as $t \to a$,

$$
\lim_{t \to a} \vec{r}(t) = \left\langle \lim_{t \to a} x(t), \lim_{t \to a} y(t), \lim_{t \to a} z(t) \right\rangle
$$

For vector-valued functions $F(t)$, $G(t)$ and real-valued function $h(t)$ with $\lim\limits_{t \to a} F(t)$, $\lim\limits_{t \to a} G(t)$, $\lim\limits_{t \to a} h(t)$ existing:

- $\lim\limits_{t \to a} [F(t) + G(t)] = \lim\limits_{t \to a} F(t) + \lim\limits_{t \to a} G(t)$
- $\lim\limits_{t \to a} [F(t) - G(t)] = \lim\limits_{t \to a} F(t) - \lim\limits_{t \to a} G(t)$
- $\lim\limits_{t \to a} [h(t)F(t)] = \left( \lim\limits_{t \to a} h(t) \right) \left( \lim\limits_{t \to a} F(t) \right)$
- $\lim\limits_{t \to a} [F(t) \cdot G(t)] = \lim\limits_{t \to a} F(t) \cdot \lim\limits_{t \to a} G(t)$
- $\lim\limits_{t \to a} [F(t) \times G(t)] = \lim\limits_{t \to a} F(t) \times \lim\limits_{t \to a} G(t)$

$\vec{r}(t)$ is continuous at $a$ **iff** $\lim\limits_{t \to a} \vec{r}(t) = \vec{r}(a)$. It is continuous on an interval $I$ if it is continuous at each point of $I$.

$\vec{r}(t)$ is continuous at $a$ **iff** each component function is continuous at $a$.

## Vector differentiation

The derivative of $\vec{r}$ is defined the same way as for a real-valued function:

$$
\vec{r}'(t) = \frac{\text{d}\vec{r}(t)}{\text{d}t} = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{\vec{r}(t+h) - \vec{r}(t)}{h}
$$

provided the limit exists.

For $\vec{r}(t) = \langle x(t), y(t), z(t) \rangle$, $\vec{r}$ is differentiable at $a$ **iff** each component function is differentiable at $a$, and

$$
\vec{r}'(a) = \langle x'(a), y'(a), z'(a) \rangle
$$

More generally, whenever the component derivatives exist,

$$
\vec{r}'(t) = \langle x'(t), y'(t), z'(t) \rangle
$$

For differentiable vector-valued functions $\vec{r}(t)$, $\vec{r}_1(t)$, $\vec{r}_2(t)$, differentiable real-valued function $f(t)$, scalar $k$, and constant vector $\vec{c}$:

- $\dfrac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}[\vec{c}] = \vec{0}$
- $\dfrac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}[\vec{r}_1(t) \pm \vec{r}_2(t)] = \vec{r}_1'(t) \pm \vec{r}_2'(t)$
- $\dfrac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}[k\vec{r}(t)] = k\vec{r}'(t)$
- $\dfrac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}[f(t)\vec{r}(t)] = f(t)\vec{r}'(t) + f'(t)\vec{r}(t)$
- $\dfrac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}[\vec{r}_1(t) \cdot \vec{r}_2(t)] = \vec{r}_1(t) \cdot \vec{r}_2'(t) + \vec{r}_1'(t) \cdot \vec{r}_2(t)$
- $\dfrac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}[\vec{r}_1(t) \times \vec{r}_2(t)] = \vec{r}_1(t) \times \vec{r}_2'(t) + \vec{r}_1'(t) \times \vec{r}_2(t)$
- $\dfrac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}[\vec{r}(f(t))] = f'(t)\vec{r}'(f(t))$

### Tangent line

Let $P$ be a point on the graph of $\vec{r}(t)$, with $\vec{r}(t_0)$ the position vector of $P$. If $\vec{r}'(t_0)$ exists and is non-zero, $\vec{r}'(t_0)$ is a tangent vector to the graph at $P$. The tangent line through $P$ parallel to $\vec{r}'(t_0)$ has vector equation

$$
\vec{x}(s) = \vec{r}(t_0) + s\vec{r}'(t_0), \qquad s \in \mathbb{R}
$$

<Note>

If $\lVert \vec{r}(t) \rVert$ is constant for all $t$ in an interval, then $\vec{r}(t) \cdot \vec{r}'(t) = 0$. So $\vec{r}(t)$ and $\vec{r}'(t)$ are orthogonal for every $t$ in the interval.

</Note>

## Definite integrals

For $\vec{r}(t) = \langle x(t), y(t), z(t) \rangle$ continuous on $[a, b]$, the definite integral over $[a, b]$ is defined componentwise:

$$
\int_a^b \vec{r}(t) \, \text{d}t = \left\langle \int_a^b x(t) \, \text{d}t, \int_a^b y(t) \, \text{d}t, \int_a^b z(t) \, \text{d}t \right\rangle
$$

For $\vec{r}(t)$, $\vec{r}_1(t)$, $\vec{r}_2(t)$ continuous on $[a, b]$ and scalar $k$:

- $\displaystyle\int_a^b k\vec{r}(t) \, \text{d}t = k \int_a^b \vec{r}(t) \, \text{d}t$
- $\displaystyle\int_a^b [\vec{r}_1(t) + \vec{r}_2(t)] \, \text{d}t = \int_a^b \vec{r}_1(t) \, \text{d}t + \int_a^b \vec{r}_2(t) \, \text{d}t$
