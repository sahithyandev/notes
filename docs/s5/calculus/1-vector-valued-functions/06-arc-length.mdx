Let $\boldsymbol{r}(t)$ be defined on $[a, b]$, and let $P = \{t_0, t_1, \dots, t_n\}$, $a = t_0 < t_1 < \dots < t_n = b$, be a partition of $[a, b]$. The polygonal length corresponding to $P$ is

$$
s(\boldsymbol{r}, P) = \sum_{k=1}^{n} \lVert \boldsymbol{r}(t_k) - \boldsymbol{r}(t_{k-1}) \rVert
$$

The curve is rectifiable **iff** these polygonal lengths have a finite least upper bound, which is the arc length of the curve.

If $x = x(t)$, $y = y(t)$, $z = z(t)$ have continuous derivatives on $[a, b]$, the arc length from $t = a$ to $t = b$ is

$$
L = \int_a^b \sqrt{\left( \frac{\text{d}x}{\text{d}t} \right)^2 + \left( \frac{\text{d}y}{\text{d}t} \right)^2 + \left( \frac{\text{d}z}{\text{d}t} \right)^2} \, \text{d}t
$$

Equivalently, for $C$ the curve traced by continuously differentiable $\boldsymbol{r}(t)$,

$$
L = \int_a^b \left\lVert \frac{\text{d}\boldsymbol{r}}{\text{d}t} \right\rVert \text{d}t
$$

<Note>

Vector-valued functions have no mean value theorem, so arc length is defined via polygonal approximations first, and only computed by the integral formula once the curve is sufficiently smooth.

</Note>

## Change of Parameter

A change of parameter in $\boldsymbol{r}(t)$ is a substitution $t = g(\tau)$ producing $\boldsymbol{r}(g(\tau))$, tracing the same curve as $\boldsymbol{r}(t)$, possibly in a different direction as $\tau$ increases.

If $\boldsymbol{r}(t)$ is differentiable with respect to $t$, and $t = g(\tau)$ is a change of parameter with $g$ differentiable with respect to $\tau$, then $\boldsymbol{r}(g(\tau))$ is differentiable with respect to $\tau$, and

$$
\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}\tau}[\boldsymbol{r}(g(\tau))] = \boldsymbol{r}'(g(\tau))g'(\tau)
$$

Equivalently,

$$
\frac{\text{d}\boldsymbol{r}}{\text{d}\tau} = \frac{\text{d}\boldsymbol{r}}{\text{d}t} \cdot \frac{\text{d}t}{\text{d}\tau}
$$

## Arc-Length Parametrization

Let $C$ be the curve traced by a continuously differentiable $\boldsymbol{r}(t)$ with $\boldsymbol{r}'(t) \ne \boldsymbol{0}$, and let $\boldsymbol{r}(t_0)$ be a reference point on $C$. The signed arc-length parameter $s$ measured from $\boldsymbol{r}(t_0)$ is

$$
s = s(t) = \int_{t_0}^{t} \left\lVert \frac{\text{d}\boldsymbol{r}}{\text{d}u} \right\rVert \text{d}u
$$

This is an odometer reading, integrating speed over time gives distance travelled.

Since $\text{d}s/\text{d}t = \lVert \boldsymbol{r}'(t) \rVert > 0$, $s = s(t)$ is strictly increasing, hence invertible into $\boldsymbol{r}(s)$. A parametrization expressed in terms of $s$ is an arc-length parametrization.

## Properties

$C$ is the curve traced by a continuously differentiable $\boldsymbol{r}(t)$ with $\boldsymbol{r}'(t) \ne \boldsymbol{0}$, and $s$ is the signed arc-length parameter defined above.

$s = s(t)$ is the arc-length parameter for $C$ **iff**

$$
\frac{\text{d}s}{\text{d}t} = \left\lVert \frac{\text{d}\boldsymbol{r}}{\text{d}t} \right\rVert
$$

This is the defining integral restated as a derivative: $s$ advances at exactly the rate $C$ is traced, not some rate tied to an arbitrary parameter $t$.

If $\boldsymbol{r}$ is expressed as a function of $s$ instead of $t$, then

$$
\left\lVert \frac{\text{d}\boldsymbol{r}}{\text{d}s} \right\rVert = 1
$$

everywhere, almost by definition: since $s$ counts distance travelled, advancing it by one unit moves $\boldsymbol{r}(s)$ exactly one unit along $C$. This is why an arc-length parametrization is aka. a **unit-speed parametrization**.

<Proof label="Proof Hint">

By the chain rule:

$$
\frac{\text{d}\boldsymbol{r}}{\text{d}t} = \frac{\text{d}\boldsymbol{r}}{\text{d}s} \cdot \frac{\text{d}s}{\text{d}t}
$$

Dividing through by $\text{d}s/\text{d}t = \lVert \boldsymbol{r}'(t) \rVert$ leaves $\lVert \text{d}\boldsymbol{r}/\text{d}s \rVert = 1$.

</Proof>

Conversely, if $\lVert \text{d}\boldsymbol{r}/\text{d}t \rVert = 1$ for all $t$, then for any $t_0$ in the domain of $\boldsymbol{r}$,

$$
s = t - t_0
$$

is already an arc-length parameter with reference point $\boldsymbol{r}(t_0)$: a unit-speed parametrization needs no reparametrization through the integral formula, only a shift so that $s = 0$ at the reference point.

## Worked Example

Find the arc length of $\boldsymbol{r}(t) = 3\cos t\,\boldsymbol{i} + 3\sin t\,\boldsymbol{j} + 4t\,\boldsymbol{k}$ for $0 \le t \le 2\pi$, with reference point $\boldsymbol{r}(0) = (3, 0, 0)$. Then find its arc-length parametrization.

<Solution>

Differentiate each component.

$$
\frac{\text{d}x}{\text{d}t} = -3\sin t, \quad \frac{\text{d}y}{\text{d}t} = 3\cos t, \quad \frac{\text{d}z}{\text{d}t} = 4
$$

Compute the speed.

$$
\left\lVert \frac{\text{d}\boldsymbol{r}}{\text{d}t} \right\rVert
= \sqrt{9\sin^2 t + 9\cos^2 t + 16} = \sqrt{25} = 5
$$

The derivatives are continuous and $\boldsymbol{r}'(t) \ne \boldsymbol{0}$, so the formula applies.

$$
L = \int_0^{2\pi} 5 \, \text{d}t = 10\pi
$$

Compute the arc-length parameter with $t_0 = 0$.

$$
s = \int_0^t 5 \, \text{d}u = 5t
$$

Invert to get $t = s/5$ and substitute into $\boldsymbol{r}$.

$$
\boldsymbol{r}(s) = 3\cos\left(\frac{s}{5}\right)\boldsymbol{i}
+ 3\sin\left(\frac{s}{5}\right)\boldsymbol{j}
+ \frac{4s}{5}\,\boldsymbol{k}
$$

Verify the unit speed.

$$
\left\lVert \frac{\text{d}\boldsymbol{r}}{\text{d}s} \right\rVert
= \sqrt{\frac{9}{25}\sin^2\left(\frac{s}{5}\right) + \frac{9}{25}\cos^2\left(\frac{s}{5}\right) + \frac{16}{25}}
= \sqrt{\frac{25}{25}} = 1
$$

The arc length is $L = 10\pi$, and the arc-length parametrization is $\boldsymbol{r}(s)$ for $0 \le s \le 10\pi$.

</Solution>

Determine the arc length of

$$
\boldsymbol{r}(t)=a\cos t\,\boldsymbol{i}+a\sin t\,\boldsymbol{j},
\qquad 0\le t\le 2\pi,
$$

where $a>0$.

<Solution>

$$
\boldsymbol{r}'(t)=-a\sin t\,\boldsymbol{i}+a\cos t\,\boldsymbol{j}
$$

The speed is constant:

$$
\left\lVert\boldsymbol{r}'(t)\right\rVert
=\sqrt{a^2\sin^2t+a^2\cos^2t}=a
$$

Therefore,

$$
L=\int_0^{2\pi}a\,\text{d}t=2\pi a
$$

</Solution>

Determine whether the graph of

$$
f(x)=x\sin\left(\frac{1}{x}\right),
\qquad 0<x\le1,
$$

with $f(0)=0$, is rectifiable on $[0,1]$.

<Solution>

For

$$
x_n=\frac{1}{\frac{\pi}{2}+n\pi},
$$

the graph passes through alternating extrema because

$$
\sin\left(\frac{1}{x_n}\right)=\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{2}+n\pi\right)=(-1)^n
$$

Therefore, $f(x_n)=(-1)^n x_n$. Consecutive extrema lie on opposite sides of the $x$-axis. The distance between consecutive graph points is at least their vertical separation:

$$
\left\lVert (x_{n+1},f(x_{n+1}))-(x_n,f(x_n))\right\rVert
\ge \left|f(x_{n+1})-f(x_n)\right|=x_{n+1}+x_n
$$

Thus, the polygonal length through these points is at least

$$
\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\left|f(x_{n+1})-f(x_n)\right|
=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}(x_{n+1}+x_n)
$$

Since

$$
x_n=\frac{1}{\pi}\cdot\frac{1}{n+\frac{1}{2}},
$$

the sum diverges by comparison with the harmonic series. The polygonal lengths are therefore unbounded, so the graph is not rectifiable.

</Solution>

Find an arc-length parametrization of the circle

$$
x^2+y^2=a^2,
\qquad a>0,
$$

with counterclockwise orientation and reference point $(a,0)$.

<Solution>

Use

$$
\boldsymbol{r}(t)=a\cos t\,\boldsymbol{i}+a\sin t\,\boldsymbol{j},
\qquad t_0=0
$$

Since $\left\lVert\boldsymbol{r}'(t)\right\rVert=a$,

$$
s=\int_0^t a\,\text{d}u=at
$$

Thus, $t=s/a$. Substitution gives

$$
\boldsymbol{r}(s)=a\cos\left(\frac{s}{a}\right)\boldsymbol{i}
+a\sin\left(\frac{s}{a}\right)\boldsymbol{j}
$$

</Solution>
