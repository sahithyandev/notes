<Theorem>

Let $\sigma$ be a [closed](/s5/calculus/vector-valued-functions/surface#closed-surface), piecewise smooth, orientable surface that encloses a solid region $V$ in $\mathbb{R}^3$. Suppose $\boldsymbol{F}$ is a [vector field](/s5/calculus/vector-valued-functions/field#vector-field) whose component functions have continuous first-order partial derivatives on an open set containing $V$ and its boundary $\sigma$. If $\hat{n}$ is the outward unit normal field for $\sigma$,

$$
\iint_\sigma \boldsymbol{F} \cdot \hat{n}\,\text{d}S = \iiint_V \text{div}\,\boldsymbol{F}\,\text{d}V
$$

</Theorem>

<DivergenceTheoremDiagram />

The left side is the total outward flux through the boundary. The right side is the total divergence inside the solid. The theorem replaces a surface integral over $\sigma$ with a triple integral over $V$.

<Formula name="Coordinate Form">

$$
\operatorname{div}\,\boldsymbol{F} = \nabla \cdot \boldsymbol{F} = \frac{\partial P}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial Q}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial R}{\partial z}
$$

  <Sym s="\boldsymbol{F}">
    $P\boldsymbol{i} + Q\boldsymbol{j} + R\boldsymbol{k}$
  </Sym>
  <Sym s="V">Solid enclosed by $\sigma$</Sym>
  <Sym s="\hat{n}">Outward unit normal</Sym>
</Symbols>

</Formula>

## Interpreting Divergence

Divergence measures the local excess of outward flow over inward flow.

- Positive divergence means that the field behaves like a source near the point.
- Negative divergence means that the field behaves like a sink near the point.
- Zero divergence means that the local outward and inward flow balance.

For a small solid $G$ centered at $P_0$,

$$
\operatorname{div}\,\boldsymbol{F}(P_0) = \lim_{G \to P_0} \frac{1}{\operatorname{Vol}(G)}\iint_{\partial G} \boldsymbol{F} \cdot \hat{n}\,\text{d}S
$$

The divergence theorem is the corresponding whole-region statement. It adds the local flux densities throughout $V$.

## Applying the Theorem

Use the following procedure when the surface is closed.

- Identify the solid $V$ enclosed by the surface.
- Check that the normal is outward.
- Compute $\operatorname{div}\,\boldsymbol{F}$.
- Set up $\iiint_V \operatorname{div}\,\boldsymbol{F}\,\text{d}V$ using convenient coordinates.
- Evaluate the triple integral.

<Note>

The theorem applies directly only to closed surfaces with outward orientation. For an open surface, add the missing faces to form a closed surface. Compute the total closed-surface flux, then subtract the flux through the added faces.

</Note>

## Examples

### Sphere

Find the outward flux of $\boldsymbol{F} = x\boldsymbol{i} + y\boldsymbol{j} + z\boldsymbol{k}$ through the sphere $x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = a^2$.

<Solution>

$$
\operatorname{div}\,\boldsymbol{F} = 1 + 1 + 1 = 3
$$

Let $V$ be the solid ball of radius $a$, with volume $\dfrac{4}{3}\pi a^3$.

$$
\Phi = \iiint_V 3\,\text{d}V = 3 \cdot \frac{4}{3}\pi a^3 = 4\pi a^3
$$

</Solution>

### Cube

Find the outward flux of $\boldsymbol{F} = x^2\boldsymbol{i} + y^2\boldsymbol{j} + z^2\boldsymbol{k}$ through the surface of the cube $0 \le x, y, z \le 1$.

<Solution>

$$
\operatorname{div}\,\boldsymbol{F} = 2x + 2y + 2z
$$

$$
\Phi = \int_0^1 \int_0^1 \int_0^1 2(x + y + z)\,\text{d}x\,\text{d}y\,\text{d}z
$$

Each term integrates to $\dfrac{1}{2}$ over the unit cube, so the 3 terms add to $2 \cdot 3 \cdot \dfrac{1}{2}$.

$$
\Phi = 3
$$

</Solution>

### Hemisphere

Find the upward flux of $\boldsymbol{F} = z\boldsymbol{k}$ through the hemisphere $x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = 1$, $z \ge 0$.

<Solution>

The hemisphere is not closed. Close it with the disk $D = \{x^2 + y^2 \le 1,\ z = 0\}$. The closed surface encloses the solid half-ball $V$.

$$
\operatorname{div}\,\boldsymbol{F} = 1
$$

$$
\iiint_V 1\,\text{d}V = \frac{1}{2} \cdot \frac{4}{3}\pi = \frac{2\pi}{3}
$$

The outward normal on $D$ is $-\boldsymbol{k}$. On $D$, $z = 0$, so

$$
\iint_D \boldsymbol{F} \cdot (-\boldsymbol{k})\,\text{d}S = \iint_D (-z)\,\text{d}S = 0
$$

The flux across the closed surface is the sum of the flux across the hemisphere and across $D$.

$$
\Phi = \frac{2\pi}{3} - 0 = \frac{2\pi}{3}
$$

</Solution>
