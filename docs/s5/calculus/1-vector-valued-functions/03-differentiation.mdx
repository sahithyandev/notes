The derivative of $\boldsymbol{r}$ is defined the same way as for a real-valued function:

$$
\boldsymbol{r}'(t) = \frac{\text{d}\boldsymbol{r}(t)}{\text{d}t} = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{\boldsymbol{r}(t+h) - \boldsymbol{r}(t)}{h}
$$

provided the limit exists.

For $\boldsymbol{r}(t) = \langle x(t), y(t), z(t) \rangle$, $\boldsymbol{r}$ is differentiable at $a$ **iff** each component function is differentiable at $a$, and

$$
\boldsymbol{r}'(a) = \langle x'(a), y'(a), z'(a) \rangle
$$

More generally, whenever the component derivatives exist,

$$
\boldsymbol{r}'(t) = \langle x'(t), y'(t), z'(t) \rangle
$$

For differentiable vector-valued functions $\boldsymbol{r}(t)$, $\boldsymbol{r}_1(t)$, $\boldsymbol{r}_2(t)$, differentiable real-valued function $f(t)$, scalar $k$, and constant vector $\boldsymbol{c}$:

- $\dfrac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}[\boldsymbol{c}] = \boldsymbol{0}$
- $\dfrac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}[\boldsymbol{r}_1(t) \pm \boldsymbol{r}_2(t)] = \boldsymbol{r}_1'(t) \pm \boldsymbol{r}_2'(t)$
- $\dfrac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}[k\boldsymbol{r}(t)] = k\boldsymbol{r}'(t)$
- $\dfrac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}[f(t)\boldsymbol{r}(t)] = f(t)\boldsymbol{r}'(t) + f'(t)\boldsymbol{r}(t)$
- $\dfrac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}[\boldsymbol{r}_1(t) \cdot \boldsymbol{r}_2(t)] = \boldsymbol{r}_1(t) \cdot \boldsymbol{r}_2'(t) + \boldsymbol{r}_1'(t) \cdot \boldsymbol{r}_2(t)$
- $\dfrac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}[\boldsymbol{r}_1(t) \times \boldsymbol{r}_2(t)] = \boldsymbol{r}_1(t) \times \boldsymbol{r}_2'(t) + \boldsymbol{r}_1'(t) \times \boldsymbol{r}_2(t)$
- $\dfrac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}[\boldsymbol{r}(f(t))] = f'(t)\boldsymbol{r}'(f(t))$

## Tangent Line

Let $P$ be a point on the graph of $\boldsymbol{r}(t)$, with $\boldsymbol{r}(t_0)$ the position vector of $P$. If $\boldsymbol{r}'(t_0)$ exists and is non-zero, $\boldsymbol{r}'(t_0)$ is a tangent vector to the graph at $P$. The tangent line through $P$ parallel to $\boldsymbol{r}'(t_0)$ has vector equation

$$
\boldsymbol{x}(s) = \boldsymbol{r}(t_0) + s\boldsymbol{r}'(t_0), \qquad s \in \mathbb{R}
$$

<Theorem level="3">

Let $\boldsymbol{r}(t)$ be differentiable on an interval. Then $\lVert \boldsymbol{r}(t) \rVert$ is constant **iff**

$$
\boldsymbol{r}(t) \cdot \boldsymbol{r}'(t) = 0
$$

for every $t$ in the interval. Thus, $\boldsymbol{r}(t)$ and $\boldsymbol{r}'(t)$ are orthogonal for every $t$ in the interval.

<Proof>

For the forward direction, suppose $\lVert \boldsymbol{r}(t) \rVert$ is constant. Square both sides:

$$
\lVert \boldsymbol{r}(t) \rVert^2 = \boldsymbol{r}(t) \cdot \boldsymbol{r}(t) = \text{constant}
$$

Differentiate with respect to $t$:

$$
\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}[\boldsymbol{r}(t) \cdot \boldsymbol{r}(t)] = 0
$$

By the product rule and commutativity of the dot product:

$$
\boldsymbol{r}'(t) \cdot \boldsymbol{r}(t) + \boldsymbol{r}(t) \cdot \boldsymbol{r}'(t) = 2\boldsymbol{r}(t) \cdot \boldsymbol{r}'(t) = 0
$$

For the converse, suppose $\boldsymbol{r}(t) \cdot \boldsymbol{r}'(t) = 0$. Then

$$
\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}\lVert \boldsymbol{r}(t) \rVert^2
= \frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}[\boldsymbol{r}(t) \cdot \boldsymbol{r}(t)]
= 2\boldsymbol{r}(t) \cdot \boldsymbol{r}'(t) = 0
$$

Therefore, $\lVert \boldsymbol{r}(t) \rVert^2$ is constant on the interval. Hence, $\lVert \boldsymbol{r}(t) \rVert$ is constant.

</Proof>

</Theorem>

## Mean Value Theorem

Vector-valued functions **do not** satisfy a direct Mean Value Theorem of the form $\boldsymbol{r}(b) - \boldsymbol{r}(a) = \boldsymbol{r}'(c)(b-a)$.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1

Find the tangent line to $\boldsymbol{r}(t)=\langle t^2,t^3,t\rangle$ at $t=1$.

<Solution>

$$
\begin{aligned}
\boldsymbol{r}(1) &= \langle1,1,1\rangle \\
\boldsymbol{r}'(t) &= \langle 2t,3t^2,1\rangle \\
\boldsymbol{r}'(1) &= \langle2,3,1\rangle
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\boldsymbol{x}(s)=\langle1,1,1\rangle+s\langle2,3,1\rangle
$$

</Solution>

### Example 2

Verify that the position and tangent vectors are orthogonal for $\boldsymbol{q}(t)=\langle\cos t,\sin t,0\rangle$.

<Solution>

$$
\boldsymbol{q}'(t)=\langle-\sin t,\cos t,0\rangle, \qquad \boldsymbol{q}(t)\cdot\boldsymbol{q}'(t)=0
$$

The position and tangent vectors are orthogonal.

</Solution>
