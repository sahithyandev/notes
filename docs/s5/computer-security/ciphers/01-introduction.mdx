An encryption algorithm ($e$), also called a cipher, transforms a plaintext ($m$) into a ciphertext ($c$) under the control of a secret key ($k$).

$$
c = e_k(m)
$$

The reverse process, decryption ($d$) or decipherment, recovers the plaintext:

$$
m = d_k(c)
$$

<Note>

Encryption and decryption algorithms ($e$ and $d$) are public. Only the key is secret, and the ciphertext ($c$) is assumed to be easily, that is publicly, accessible.

</Note>

### Secret Key

The secrecy of plaintext $m$ is totally dependent on the secrecy of the key $k$.

Algorithms with this property are called symmetric cryptosystems or secret key cryptosystems. Both parties to a secret communication must know $k$ and keep it secret.

## Categorization

The below categories are not mutually exclusive, and modern ciphers often fall into multiple categories.

### Classical Ciphers

The ciphers used in the pre-computer era are called historical ciphers or classical ciphers.

Historical ciphers used plaintext linked to:

- A particular language.
- The alphabet of symbols or characters used.
- The peculiar statistical characteristics of the language.

For English:

- A 26 character alphabet (A to Z).
- Higher usage of characters e, t, a, o, i.
- Higher appearance of bigrams (th, he, an, in) and trigrams (the, ing, and, her, ere).

### Mono-alphabetic Ciphers

A plaintext character is replaced with a ciphertext character according to a fixed rule.

Preserves the language's statistical characteristics in the ciphertext.

The [shift](/s5/computer-security/ciphers/shift-cipher) and [substitution](/s5/computer-security/ciphers/substitution-cipher) ciphers are examples of mono-alphabetic ciphers.

### Poly-alphabetic Ciphers

Uses multiple substitution alphabets, e.g. one alphabet for odd-numbered positions and a different one for even-numbered positions, flattening the character frequency graph.

Using $n$ alphabets in a poly-alphabetic cipher gives a key space of $(26!)^n$ at the cost of remembering a key of length $26n$ characters.
