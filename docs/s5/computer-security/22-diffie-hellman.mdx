Diffie-Hellman (DH), invented in 1976 by Whitfield Diffie and Martin Hellman, lets 2 parties agree on a shared secret key using only publicly exchanged information. Security rests on the difficulty of computing discrete logarithms.

This is the Discrete Logarithm Problem (DLP) over the finite field $\mathbb{Z}_p^*$: recovering the secret $a$ from $g^a \bmod p$ is computationally intractable for large $p$, while computing $g^a \bmod p$ from $a$ is cheap.

The [interactive guide to Diffie-Hellman key exchange](https://sahithyan.dev/post/diffie-hellman-key-exchange) walks through the exchange and the discrete logarithm problem behind it.

<Note>

DH is a key agreement scheme: both parties contribute equally to the shared key. RSA, by contrast, is a key delivery scheme.

</Note>

The protocol has 4 parts: define public parameters, generate key pairs, exchange public keys, compute the shared secret.

## Public Parameters

- A large prime modulus $p$, defining the multiplicative group $\mathbb{Z}_p^*$.
- A generator $g$, a primitive root modulo $p$.

$g$ is a primitive root modulo $p$ **iff** every $a$ coprime to $p$ satisfies $a \equiv g^i \pmod p$ for some integer $i$, called the discrete logarithm of $a$ to base $g$.

For prime $p$, the $p - 1$ values $i = 1, \dots, p-1$ generate $\{1, \dots, p-1\}$, in some order, via $g^i \bmod p$.

Choosing $g$ as a primitive root maximises the order of the group it generates to $p - 1$, so its powers span every non-zero residue class modulo $p$ and stay in no small subgroup, where the discrete logarithm would be easy to solve.

### Choosing a Modulus

$p$ is chosen as a safe prime: a prime of the form $p = 2q + 1$ where $q$ is also prime.

Here:

- $q$: the Sophie Germain prime paired with $p$

Reasons:

- Pohlig-Hellman reduces the discrete logarithm modulo $p$ to one discrete logarithm per prime factor of $p - 1$. A safe prime forces $p - 1 = 2q$, leaving only the factors $2$ and the large prime $q$.
- No small-order subgroup exists, so an attacker cannot confine the shared secret to a short list of values.
- Every $g \neq \pm 1$ has order $q$ or $2q$, so almost any candidate generates a large group.

### Choosing a Generator

Factor $p - 1$ into its distinct prime factors $q_1, \dots, q_k$. Then $g$ is a primitive root modulo $p$ **iff**

$$
g^{(p-1)/q_i} \not\equiv 1 \pmod p \quad \text{for every } i.
$$

Here:

- $q_i$: a distinct prime factor of $p - 1$

Test $g = 2, 3, 5, \dots$ in turn. Primitive roots are dense, so a small $g$ passes after few tries. Each candidate costs $k$ modular exponentiations.

Factoring $p - 1$ is the hard step, so $p$ is chosen in advance to keep it known.

A safe prime is a prime $p = 2q + 1$ where $q$ is also prime. Then $p - 1 = 2q$ has 2 prime factors, so $g$ is a primitive root **iff** $g^2 \not\equiv 1 \pmod p$ **and** $g^q \not\equiv 1 \pmod p$. Published standards use safe primes and usually take $g = 2$.

For $p = 23$, $p - 1 = 22 = 2 \cdot 11$. Testing $g = 5$ gives $5^2 \equiv 2$ and $5^{11} \equiv 22$. Neither is $1$, so $5$ is a primitive root. $g = 2$ fails, since $2^{11} \equiv 1 \pmod{23}$.

<Note>

These parameters are generated once by a trusted authority, e.g. NIST, and published as a standard. Individual users incur no cost for parameter generation.

</Note>

## Key Pair Generation

Each party chooses a secret integer and publishes its exponentiation modulo $p$.

- Alice chooses secret $a \in \mathbb{Z}_p^*$, computes public $A \equiv g^a \pmod p$.
- Bob chooses secret $b \in \mathbb{Z}_p^*$, computes public $B \equiv g^b \pmod p$.

## Exchange of Public Keys

Public keys need no confidentiality, only authenticity and integrity, since a forged public key lets an attacker substitute their own key undetected.

Methods: a trusted public key server, in-person or verified exchange, or a CA-issued digital certificate.

## Shared Secret Key

Alice computes $k = B^a \bmod p = g^{ba} \bmod p$. Bob computes $k = A^b \bmod p = g^{ab} \bmod p$. Both arrive at the same value since $g^{ab} \equiv g^{ba} \pmod p$.

### Worked Example

$p = 23$, $g = 5$, $a = 6$, $b = 15$.

- $A = 5^6 \bmod 23$.
- $B = 5^{15} \bmod 23$.
- Alice computes $k = B^6 \bmod 23 = 2$.
- Bob computes $k = A^{15} \bmod 23 = 2$.

## Ephemeral Diffie-Hellman

Ephemeral Diffie-Hellman (DHE) generates a fresh key pair for every session and deletes both private keys once the shared secret is set up. Long-term keys authenticate the exchange only, they do not derive the session key.

This gives perfect forward secrecy: if the long-term keys are stolen later, past session keys stay safe, because the private keys that produced them no longer exist and cannot be recovered from the recorded public values.

### Static DH

Both parties reuse the same key pair across every session instead of generating a fresh one.

- A single compromised private key exposes every past and future session, since the same $a$ or $b$ derives every session's shared secret.
- Costs 1 exponentiation total per party, instead of 1 per session.

### Authenticating the Ephemeral Keys

Raw DH has no identity binding, so a fresh, unsigned $A$ or $B$ is exactly what a [man-in-the-middle](#man-in-the-middle-attack) substitutes. DHE fixes this by signing the ephemeral public key with a separate long-term signing key, not by deriving the session key from it.

- Alice signs $A$ with her long-term private signing key, sends $(A, \text{signature})$.
- Bob verifies the signature against Alice's long-term public key, then proceeds with $A$ exactly as in plain DH.

<Note>

TLS cipher suites name this split explicitly. `DHE_RSA` signs the ephemeral exchange with a long-term RSA key, `DHE_DSS` with DSA, `ECDHE_RSA` and `ECDHE_ECDSA` the elliptic-curve analogue. The long-term key authenticates $A$ and $B$, it never enters the shared secret computation.

</Note>

Why forward secrecy holds:

- $a$ and $b$ are generated fresh per session and destroyed once $k$ is computed.
- Recovering $a$ from the recorded public $A$ requires solving the DLP, not merely knowing the long-term signing key.
- A long-term key compromised later lets an attacker forge future signatures, but supplies no way to recompute a past session's $a$ or $b$.

## Man-in-the-Middle Attack

The basic DH protocol has no authentication of public keys, so it is vulnerable to a man-in-the-middle attack.

<Note title="Proof Hint">

- Alice sends her public key $A$ toward Bob.
- Mallory intercepts, and forwards her own public key $A'$ to Bob instead, claiming it is Alice's.
- Bob computes a shared key $k_1$ with Mallory, believing it is shared with Alice.
- Bob sends his public key $B$ toward Alice.
- Mallory intercepts, and forwards her own public key $B'$ to Alice instead, claiming it is Bob's.
- Alice computes a shared key $k_2$ with Mallory, believing it is shared with Bob.

</Note>

Mallory now holds a separate shared key with each party, and can decrypt, inspect, modify, or inject messages in the supposedly secure channel between Alice and Bob.

### Preventing the Attack

The attack works because $A$ and $B$ carry no proof of who sent them. Every fix binds the exchanged keys to a verified identity.

- Digital signature  
  Sign the exchanged public key with a long-term signing key, as in [ephemeral DH authentication](#authenticating-the-ephemeral-keys). Bob verifies the signature before trusting $A$.
- Certificate  
  A CA-issued certificate binds a long-term public key to an identity, letting Bob verify Alice's signing key without a prior direct exchange with her.
- Station-to-Station protocol  
  Each party signs both exchanged values $A$ and $B$ together and encrypts the signature under the just-derived shared key. Mallory's substituted keys cannot produce a valid signature over the real $A$ and $B$.
- Out-of-band verification  
  Compare a short fingerprint of the exchanged keys over a separate trusted channel, e.g. reading it aloud on a phone call.

<Note>

Every fix needs a trust anchor Mallory cannot also forge or intercept, a long-term signing key, a CA, or a side channel. Plain DH supplies none of these on its own.

</Note>
