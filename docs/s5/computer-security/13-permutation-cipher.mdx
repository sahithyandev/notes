One specific [classical](/s5/computer-security/ciphers#classical-cipher) cipher in the [transposition](/s5/computer-security/ciphers#transposition-cipher) category. The category as a whole is also called permutation ciphers. This note covers the concrete construction that carries the name, in the same way the [shift cipher](/s5/computer-security/shift-cipher) is one concrete cipher in the mono-alphabetic category.

Reorders the plaintext letters rather than substituting them, so every single-letter frequency is preserved unchanged.

Requires a block length $n$, typically small, and a permutation $\sigma \in S_n$ as the secret key.

$$
\sigma = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 & 4 & 5 \\ 2 & 4 & 1 & 3 & 5 \end{pmatrix}
$$

The bottom row gives $\sigma(i)$ for each input position $i$ in the top row. Here $\sigma(1) = 2$, $\sigma(2) = 4$, $\sigma(3) = 1$, $\sigma(4) = 3$, $\sigma(5) = 5$.

## Encryption

Split the plaintext into blocks of $n$ characters. Pad the final block with filler characters if it is short.

Within each block, the character at position $i$ moves to position $\sigma(i)$.

$$
c_{\sigma(i)} = m_i
$$

Here:

- $m_i$: plaintext character at position $i$ in the block
- $c_j$: ciphertext character at position $j$ in the block

## Decryption

The receiver knows $\sigma$. Within each block, position $i$ of the plaintext is read from position $\sigma(i)$ of the ciphertext.

$$
m_i = c_{\sigma(i)}
$$

This is encryption under the inverse permutation $\sigma^{-1}$.

## Attacking

Single-character frequencies in the ciphertext match the standard language distribution, since the permutation only moves characters. A ciphertext whose frequencies match the standard distribution but which does not decrypt as a substitution cipher points to a permutation cipher.

The attack recovers $n$ first, then $\sigma$.

Recovering the block length $n$:

- A plaintext word that repeats at the same offset within its block is permuted the same way each time, so it produces an identical character group in the ciphertext.
- Measure the gap between the start positions of 2 such repeated groups. The gap is a multiple of $n$.
- Take the gcd of several such gaps. That gcd is $n$, or a small multiple of it.

Recovering the permutation $\sigma$:

- Cut the ciphertext into blocks of length $n$.
- Try each of the $n!$ candidate permutations, applying its inverse to every block.
- Keep the candidate that turns every block into readable text.

## Worked Example

Key: $\sigma(1) = 2$, $\sigma(2) = 4$, $\sigma(3) = 1$, $\sigma(4) = 3$, $\sigma(5) = 5$. Plaintext HELLO, one block of 5.

Encryption places $m_i$ at position $\sigma(i)$:

- $m_1 = \text{H} \to$ position 2
- $m_2 = \text{E} \to$ position 4
- $m_3 = \text{L} \to$ position 1
- $m_4 = \text{L} \to$ position 3
- $m_5 = \text{O} \to$ position 5

Reading positions 1 to 5: L, H, L, E, O.

Ciphertext: LHLEO.

Decryption reads $m_i$ from position $\sigma(i)$:

- $m_1 = c_2 = \text{H}$
- $m_2 = c_4 = \text{E}$
- $m_3 = c_1 = \text{L}$
- $m_4 = c_3 = \text{L}$
- $m_5 = c_5 = \text{O}$

Plaintext: HELLO.

The attack starts from ciphertext blocks with the block length unknown.

- The group `LHL` recurs, starting at positions 1 and 11. The gap $11 - 1 = 10$ is a multiple of $n$. A second repeated group gives a gap of $15$. Their gcd is $5$, so $n = 5$.
- Cut the ciphertext into blocks of 5.
- Each of the $5! = 120$ candidate permutations is applied in inverse to every block.
- The candidate $\sigma(1) = 2$, $\sigma(2) = 4$, $\sigma(3) = 1$, $\sigma(4) = 3$, $\sigma(5) = 5$ turns every block into readable text and is kept.
