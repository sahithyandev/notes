A method of constructing new data points within the range of a discrete set of known data points. The constructed function passes through all given points.

## Weierstrass Approximation Theorem

Suppose $f$ is continuous on $[a,b]$. Then $\forall \epsilon > 0$, $\exists$ a polynomial $P(x)$ such that $\forall x \in [a,b]$:

$$
\left\lvert f(x) - P(x) \right\rvert < \epsilon
$$

<Note>

For $n + 1$ data points, exactly one polynomial of degree $\leq n$ passes through all points.

</Note>

## Types

### Linear Interpolation

Fits a straight line between 2 adjacent data points $(x_0, y_0)$ and $(x_1, y_1)$:

$$
P(x) = y_0 + \frac{y_1 - y_0}{x_1 - x_0}(x - x_0)
$$

Simple but only accurate when the underlying function is nearly linear between nodes.

### Polynomial Interpolation

Fits a single polynomial of degree $n$ through $n + 1$ data points. Methods include [Lagrange](/s2/methods-of-mathematics/numerical-methods/lagrange-interpolating-polynomials/) and [Newton's divided differences](/s2/methods-of-mathematics/numerical-methods/newton-interpolating-polynomials/).

High-degree polynomials can oscillate wildly near the endpoints (Runge's phenomenon).

### Spline Interpolation

Fits piecewise polynomials between consecutive data points, joined smoothly at **knots**. Cubic splines are most common: each piece is degree 3 and the join points satisfy $C^2$ continuity (matching function value, first derivative, and second derivative).

Avoids Runge's phenomenon while remaining smooth.
