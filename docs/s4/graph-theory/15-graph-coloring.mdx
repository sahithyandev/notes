The assignment of colors to vertices (or edges) of a graph such that no 2 adjacent vertices share the same color.

## Chromatic Number

The chromatic number $\chi(G)$ is the minimum number of colors needed to properly color the vertices of $G$.

Bounds:

- $\chi(G) \ge 1$ for any non-empty graph
- $\chi(G) \ge \omega(G)$, where $\omega(G)$ is the clique number (size of the largest complete subgraph)
- $\chi(G) \le \Delta(G) + 1$, where $\Delta(G)$ is the maximum vertex degree (Brooks' theorem tightens this for non-complete non-odd-cycle graphs to $\chi(G) \le \Delta(G)$)

Special cases:

- $\chi(G) = 1$ **iff** $G$ has no edges
- $\chi(G) = 2$ **iff** $G$ is bipartite
- $\chi(K_n) = n$ for the complete graph on $n$ vertices

## Four-Color Theorem

Any planar graph satisfies $\chi(G) \le 4$. The converse does not hold: a graph with $\chi(G) \le 4$ need not be planar.

## Algorithms

Determining $\chi(G)$ exactly is NP-hard.

- Greedy coloring assigns colors in vertex order, using the smallest color not taken by any neighbor. Produces at most $\Delta(G) + 1$ colors. Not globally optimal.
- Backtracking search finds the exact chromatic number but is exponential in the worst case.
- Heuristic methods (DSATUR, RLF) give near-optimal colorings for large graphs.

## Applications

- Register allocation in compilers: variables are vertices, edges connect variables live simultaneously, colors are registers.
- Scheduling: tasks are vertices, conflicts are edges, colors are time slots.
- Map coloring: regions are vertices, shared borders are edges.
