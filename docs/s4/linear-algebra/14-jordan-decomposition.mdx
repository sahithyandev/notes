The process of decomposing a square matrix $A$ into this form:

$$
A = PJP^{-1}
$$

Here:

- $J$ is the Jordan form of $A$
- $P$ is an invertible matrix where the columns are generalized eigenvectors of $A$

Generalizes diagonalization when matrix is not diagonalizable. Every square matrix $A$ is Jordan-decomposable.

## Jordan Block

Denoted by $B(\lambda,m)$. An upper triangular matrix $B_{m\times m}$ is a Jordan block **iff**:

- All $m$ diagonal elements are the same eigenvalue ($\lambda$) and
- All super-diagonal elements are $1$

$$
B(\lambda, m) =
\begin{pmatrix}
\lambda & 1 & 0 & \cdots & 0 & 0 \\
\vdots  & \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots & \vdots \\
0 & 0 & 0 & \cdots & \lambda & 1 \\
0 & 0 & 0 & \cdots & 0 & \lambda
\end{pmatrix}
$$

## Jordan Form

Aka. Jordan canonical form. $J$ from the decomposition.

$$
J = \text{diag}(B(λ_1, m_1), B(λ_2, m_2), \dots , B(λ_k, m_k))
$$

Here:

- $\lambda_i$ is an eigenvalue of $A$ for $i=1,2,\dots,m$
- $m_i \in \mathbb{R}$ where $\sum m_i = n$

Unique for a given matrix, up to the reordering of the Jordan blocks.

2 matrices $A$ and $B$ are similar **iff** they have the same Jordan form (up to the reordering of the Jordan blocks).

## Finding Jordan Form

**Step 1:** Find the characteristic polynomial $f(\lambda) = \det(\lambda I - A)$.

**Step 2:** Solve $f(\lambda) = 0$. Each distinct root is an eigenvalue. The power of $(\lambda - \lambda_i)$ in $f$ is $a_{\lambda_i}$.

**Step 3:** For each eigenvalue $\lambda_i$, compute:

- $g_{\lambda_i} = n - \text{rank}(A - \lambda_i I)$  
  Number of Jordan blocks for $\lambda_i$.
- $a_{\lambda_i}$  
  Sum of sizes of all blocks for $\lambda_i$.

**Step 4:** Determine the sizes of the $g_\lambda$ blocks, which must sum to $a_\lambda$.

If $a_\lambda = g_\lambda$: all blocks are $1 \times 1$.

Otherwise, compute $r_k = \text{rank}(A - \lambda I)^k$ for $k = 0, 1, 2, \dots$

Number of blocks of size $\ge k$ is:

$$
n_{\ge k} = r_{k-1} - r_k
$$

Number of blocks of size exactly $k$ is:

$$
n_k = r_{k-1} - 2r_k + r_{k+1}
$$

<Note>

For $n \le 3$, $m_\lambda = a_\lambda - g_\lambda + 1$ (from the relationship $a_\lambda - g_\lambda = m_\lambda - 1$). This gives the largest block size directly.

</Note>

**Step 5:** Assemble $J$ by placing each Jordan block $B(\lambda_i, k)$ along the diagonal.

## Relationship between multiplicities

Suppose Jordan blocks of the eigenvalue $\lambda$ are of the sizes $k_1,k_2,\dots,k_r \ge 1$:

- $g_\lambda = r$
  Number of Jordan blocks associated with $\lambda$ is equal to the geometric multiplicity of $\lambda$.
- $a_λ = \sum_{i=1}^{r} k_i \ge r$
  Algebraic multiplicity of $\lambda$ is equal to the sum of sizes of the Jordan blocks. And it's greater than the number of Jordan blocks. Obvious.
- $m_\lambda = \text{max}(k_1, k_2,\dots, k_r)$
- $g_\lambda (m_\lambda - 1) \ge a_\lambda - g_\lambda = \sum_{i=1}^{r} {k_i - 1} \ge m_\lambda - 1$
- $a_\lambda-g_\lambda = m_\lambda-1$ if the size of the matrix $n\le3$
