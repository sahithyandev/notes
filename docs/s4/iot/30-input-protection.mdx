## Input Protection and Clamping

MCU GPIO input pins have absolute maximum voltage ratings, typically $V_{CC} + 0.3\ \text{V}$ to $V_{CC} + 0.5\ \text{V}$ above the supply and $-0.3\ \text{V}$ to $-0.5\ \text{V}$ below GND. Exceeding these limits permanently damages the ESD protection structures or input transistors. Signals from external sources can carry voltages outside this safe window due to inductive spikes, ground offsets, or wiring errors.

A clamp circuit limits the voltage reaching the pin to within the safe range regardless of what the external signal does.

### Series Resistor

A resistor in series with the input pin limits the current flowing into the pin's internal ESD diodes when the voltage exceeds the safe range. Without a series resistor, an overvoltage event drives unlimited current through the ESD diodes, destroying them even if they clamp the voltage.

Typical value: $1\ \text{k}\Omega$–$10\ \text{k}\Omega$. Higher values give more current limiting but slow the input edge (RC with pin capacitance).

### Diode Clamp to Supply Rails

2 diodes, 1 to $V_{CC}$ and 1 to GND, clamp the input to within 1 diode drop of each rail.

- $D_1$: anode at signal, cathode at $V_{CC}$. Conducts when $V_{signal} > V_{CC} + V_f$, pulling the pin back toward $V_{CC}$.
- $D_2$: anode at GND, cathode at signal. Conducts when $V_{signal} < -V_f$, clamping reverse-polarity signals.

Schottky diodes are preferred. Forward voltage $V_f \approx 0.3\ \text{V}$ vs $0.7\ \text{V}$ for silicon. The clamped voltage stays closer to the rail, reducing stress on the pin. Schottky diodes also respond faster to transients.

The series resistor must be present to limit clamping current. Without it, a sustained overvoltage forces the $V_{CC}$ rail upward through $D_1$, potentially damaging other devices on the same supply.

### Zener Diode Clamp

A zener diode from signal to GND (cathode at signal, anode at GND) clamps at its zener breakdown voltage $V_Z$.

Choose $V_Z$ just below the pin's absolute maximum. For a $3.3\ \text{V}$ MCU, a $3.6\ \text{V}$ zener clamps at $3.6\ \text{V}$ while passing $3.3\ \text{V}$ signals undisturbed.

Clamps overvoltage but does not protect against reverse polarity on its own. A second zener or a standard diode to GND handles the negative direction.

### TVS Diode

A Transient Voltage Suppressor is optimised for high-energy transients (ESD, inductive kickback). It behaves like a very fast zener with a higher peak power rating.

- Unidirectional TVS: clamps 1 polarity, like a zener.
- Bidirectional TVS: clamps both overvoltage and reverse polarity in a single component.

TVS diodes are the preferred solution when the signal line may carry ESD events or inductive spikes from motors, solenoids, or long cables. A series resistor is still required to limit sustained current.

<Note>

Internal ESD diodes exist on most MCU inputs but are rated only for ESD events (a few hundred nanoseconds). They are not designed to sink sustained overcurrent. External protection is required any time the signal source can maintain an out-of-range voltage for more than a microsecond.

</Note>

## Fuses

A fuse is a sacrificial overcurrent protection device placed in series with a power supply line. When current exceeds the fuse's rated value, it opens the circuit, disconnecting the load before sustained overcurrent can damage components.

Without a fuse, a short circuit or component failure can drive unlimited current through the PCB traces, MCU, and other components. Traces act as resistors. Sufficient current generates enough heat to delaminate the PCB, destroy the MCU, or start a fire.

### Fuse Operation

A fuse contains a conductor sized to melt (or increase dramatically in resistance) at its trip current. The energy deposited in the conductor is $I^2 \times R \times t$. When this energy exceeds the thermal capacity of the conductor, it opens.

Key ratings:

- Current rating $I_F$  
  Continuous current the fuse carries without tripping. Rated slightly above the maximum normal operating current of the circuit.
- Voltage rating  
  Maximum voltage the open fuse can safely block. A fuse that opens under a voltage exceeding its rating may arc across the gap.
- Breaking capacity  
  Maximum fault current the fuse can safely interrupt. At very high fault currents, the arc during opening may sustain conduction.

### Fast-Blow vs Slow-Blow

- Fast-blow (FF)  
  Opens very quickly at the rated current. Protects sensitive components that cannot tolerate even brief overcurrent. Used for MCUs, logic ICs, and signal circuitry.
- Slow-blow (T, time-delay)  
  Tolerates brief inrush currents above the rated value before tripping. Opens only on sustained overcurrent. Used for motors, transformers, and capacitive loads where normal startup draws a spike above steady-state current.

Using a fast-blow fuse on a motor input causes nuisance tripping on every startup. Using a slow-blow fuse on an MCU power rail allows sustained overcurrent to damage the MCU before the fuse opens.

### One-Time vs Resettable Fuses

- One-time fuse  
  The conductor melts permanently. Must be physically replaced after a fault. Used in production devices where fuse replacement signals a genuine fault requiring inspection.
- Resettable fuse (PTC, polyfuse)  
  A polymer positive temperature coefficient device. Under overcurrent, it heats up and its resistance increases by orders of magnitude, reducing current to a safe trickle. When power is removed and the device cools, it resets to low resistance automatically.

Resettable fuse properties:

- No replacement needed after a trip.
- Trip current is less precise than a one-time fuse.
- Small residual resistance even in normal operation ($0.1\ \Omega$–$1\ \Omega$).
- Common in USB ports, battery packs, and IoT development boards.

<Note>

In IoT devices, a polyfuse on the USB 5 V input and a fast-blow fuse on the battery output are typical. The polyfuse handles accidental shorts during development. The battery fuse prevents thermal runaway in case of cell damage.

</Note>
