## Pull-Up and Pull-Down Resistors

A floating input pin has no external driver and reads an undefined voltage. The MCU input register samples whatever noise or leakage current is present, producing unpredictable HIGH or LOW readings.

A pull resistor fixes this by tying the pin to a known voltage through a high-value resistor (typically $1\ \text{k}\Omega$–$100\ \text{k}\Omega$).

- Pull-up resistor  
  Connects the pin to $V_{CC}$. The pin reads HIGH when nothing drives it. A device that pulls the line LOW can override the resistor.
- Pull-down resistor  
  Connects the pin to GND. The pin reads LOW when nothing drives it. A device that pulls the line HIGH overrides the resistor.

## Resistor Value Trade-off

The pull resistor value must satisfy 2 conflicting constraints.

The lower bound is set by current. When an external device drives the line to the opposite rail, current flows through the pull resistor from $V_{CC}$ to GND. For a pull-up with the line driven LOW:

$$
I = \frac{V_{CC}}{R}
$$

The current flows continuously while the line is asserted. A low resistance:

- Wastes power. Critical in battery-powered IoT devices.
- Stresses the driving transistor, which must sink the full resistor current in addition to any load current.
- Heats the resistor.

The resistor must be high enough to keep current within the drive capability of the weakest device on the line.

The upper bound is set by voltage integrity. A pull resistor and the parasitic capacitance of the wire, pin, and connected devices form an RC circuit. When the line is released, the resistor must charge the parasitic capacitance $C_{line}$ back to the rail:

$$
\tau = R \times C_{line}
$$

A high resistance:

- Slows the rising edge. The line takes many time constants to reach $V_{IH}$.
- Extends the forbidden band crossing duration, causing metastability or spurious transitions at the receiver.
- Prevents the line from fully reaching the rail before the next transition at high signal frequencies, reducing noise margins.

Any leakage current produces a voltage error across a high resistance:

$$
V_{error} = I_{leakage} \times R
$$

A $100\ \text{nA}$ leakage across a $100\ \text{k}\Omega$ resistor produces a $10\ \text{mV}$ offset. The same leakage across $1\ \text{M}\Omega$ produces $100\ \text{mV}$, which can push the line into the forbidden band on a 3.3 V system.

Typical values for most applications are $1\ \text{k}\Omega$ to $10\ \text{k}\Omega$:

- $1\ \text{k}\Omega$ to $4.7\ \text{k}\Omega$: I²C buses and higher-speed signals (fast edges required).
- $4.7\ \text{k}\Omega$ to $10\ \text{k}\Omega$: general-purpose GPIO, buttons, slow signals.

Values above $10\ \text{k}\Omega$ are only suitable for ultra-low-power designs where signal speed is not a concern. Values below $1\ \text{k}\Omega$ draw excessive current.

Most MCUs include configurable internal pull-up resistors (and sometimes pull-down) that can be enabled in software, eliminating the need for external components on simple inputs. Internal pull-ups are typically $20\ \text{k}\Omega$–$50\ \text{k}\Omega$, suitable for buttons but too high for I²C or high-speed lines.

## Why Pull-Up Resistors Are Required for MCU Inputs

- Undefined floating state  
  An undriven MCU input has no connection to either rail. It picks up environmental noise and reads unpredictably. A pull-up clamps the idle voltage to $V_{CC}$, giving a defined HIGH when nothing actively drives the line.
- No internal drive on digital inputs  
  MCU input buffers are high-impedance. Any voltage, including the forbidden band, is a legal input to the pin hardware. Only the pull resistor enforces a valid default.
- RC low-pass filter interaction  
  Any capacitance on the input line forms an RC low-pass filter with the pull-up resistor. A large pull-up value combined with significant capacitance can round edges so severely that the signal lingers in the forbidden band, causing metastability. Pull-up values should stay within $1\ \text{k}\Omega$–$10\ \text{k}\Omega$. Unnecessary capacitance on input lines should be avoided.
- Safe default on device disconnect  
  If the connected sensor or peripheral loses power or is physically removed, the pull-up ensures the input stays at a known HIGH.

A floating input pin has input impedance in the megaohm range. The high impedance makes the pin voltage extremely sensitive to stray charge.

The pin, its internal traces, and the ESD protection circuitry form a small parasitic capacitance. Charge accumulates from:

- Electromagnetic fields from nearby switching circuits (power supplies, motors, other ICs)
- Capacitive coupling from adjacent PCB traces
- RF signals in the environment

With input impedance this high, even a tiny induced current builds enough voltage across the parasitic capacitance to exceed $V_{IH}$. No low-impedance path exists to drain the accumulated charge, so the pin voltage stays in the HIGH region.

Most MCUs have weak internal pull-up resistors, or parasitic leakage paths from $V_{CC}$ through ESD protection diodes and input buffer transistors. These structures bias the floating pin toward $V_{CC}$ rather than GND.

CMOS thresholds are asymmetric. $V_{IH} \approx 0.7 \times V_{CC}$. The HIGH region spans a wide voltage range, so any upward drift easily lands in it. The input flip-flop samples the pin on each clock cycle. With the voltage in the HIGH region, it latches a stable 1 for as long as nothing actively pulls the pin LOW.

A pull-down resistor to GND provides a low-resistance path that drains induced charge and holds the pin at LOW by default. A pull-up to $V_{CC}$ defines the default as HIGH and only transitions LOW when a device actively drives the line there.

## Logic Level Thresholds and the Forbidden Band

Every logic family defines 4 voltage thresholds:

- $V_{OL}$: maximum voltage an output guarantees when driving LOW
- $V_{OH}$: minimum voltage an output guarantees when driving HIGH
- $V_{IL}$: maximum voltage an input will reliably interpret as LOW
- $V_{IH}$: minimum voltage an input will reliably interpret as HIGH

The region between $V_{IL}$ and $V_{IH}$ is the forbidden band. A signal in this range is neither a valid LOW nor a valid HIGH. The input's output state is undefined. It may oscillate, latch at an intermediate voltage, or resolve differently on each device.

No signal should ever sit in the forbidden band in steady state. The noise margins ($V_{IL} - V_{OL}$ for LOW, $V_{OH} - V_{IH}$ for HIGH) quantify how much voltage deviation the interface tolerates before entering the forbidden band.

The threshold voltage $V_{th}$ is the nominal switching point of a gate. It lies inside the forbidden band.

$V_{th}$ depends on the logic family:

- CMOS: $V_{th} \approx V_{CC}/2$. Scales with supply voltage. Symmetric noise margins.
- TTL: $V_{th} \approx 1.4\ \text{V}$. Fixed regardless of supply. Asymmetric noise margins (LOW margin is narrower).

$V_{th}$ is not a safe operating point. A signal held exactly at $V_{th}$ leaves the output in a high-gain linear region, consuming maximum current and producing maximum noise. The forbidden band exists because $V_{th}$ has manufacturing spread. No 2 gates switch at exactly the same voltage, and temperature shifts it further.

Signals pass through the forbidden band during transitions. A fast edge spends negligible time there. A slow edge, from a long cable, RC load, or weak driver, lingers in the band, causing the receiver to toggle unpredictably. Schmitt trigger inputs solve this.

## Schmitt Trigger Inputs

A standard digital input has a single switching threshold. A signal that crosses it slowly causes the output to toggle multiple times as noise kicks it back and forth across the threshold.

A Schmitt trigger input replaces the single $V_{th}$ with 2 thresholds:

- $V_{T+}$: upper threshold. Output switches HIGH when input rises above this.
- $V_{T-}$: lower threshold. Output switches LOW when input falls below this.

The hysteresis band is the voltage interval $[V_{T-},\ V_{T+}]$. Its width is:

$$
V_H = V_{T+} - V_{T-}
$$

Switching behaviour is history-dependent:

- If the output is currently LOW, it switches HIGH only when input exceeds $V_{T+}$.
- If the output is currently HIGH, it switches LOW only when input drops below $V_{T-}$.
- While the input is inside $[V_{T-},\ V_{T+}]$, the output holds its last state regardless of input movement.

The transfer characteristic forms a loop rather than a single crossing point. The same input voltage can correspond to 2 different output states depending on which direction the signal arrived from. Noise smaller than $V_H$ cannot cause any transition regardless of where it occurs on the input.

A standard gate has 1 $V_{th}$. Any noise that crosses it toggles the output. A Schmitt trigger requires the signal to travel the full width of the hysteresis band before the output changes.

Schmitt trigger inputs are tolerant of:

- Slow-rising signals (RC-charged lines, long cables)
- Noisy environments (motors, switching regulators near signal lines)
- Mechanical switch bounce

Many MCU GPIO pins have a Schmitt trigger option that can be enabled per pin. UART RX, external interrupt pins, and I²C lines typically use Schmitt trigger inputs by default.

## Open-Collector Outputs

A standard push-pull output drives the pin actively HIGH (connecting it to $V_{CC}$ through a transistor) or actively LOW (connecting it to GND through a transistor). Both rails are driven.

An open-collector output has only the LOW-side transistor. It can pull the line to GND (asserted) or release it entirely (Hi-Z). It cannot actively drive the line HIGH.

To produce a usable HIGH level, an external pull-up resistor connects the line to $V_{CC}$. When no device asserts the line, the resistor pulls it HIGH passively.

Open-collector is used for:

- Wired-AND bus  
  Multiple open-collector devices share 1 wire. Any device can pull the line LOW independently. The line is HIGH only when all devices release it. I²C uses this: any device can hold SDA or SCL LOW to stall the bus, enabling clock stretching and multi-master arbitration.
- Voltage level shifting  
  The pull-up resistor connects to any supply voltage regardless of the MCU's own logic level. A 3.3 V MCU can drive a 5 V bus by using a 5 V pull-up. The open-collector transistor only needs to pull to GND.
- Short-circuit safety  
  If 2 devices simultaneously assert conflicting levels on a push-pull bus, a near-short circuit results. On an open-collector bus, 2 simultaneous LOW assertions are harmless. Both transistors pull to GND together.

<Note>

Open-drain is the MOSFET equivalent of open-collector and is the term used in modern datasheets. The behaviour is identical.

</Note>
