A technique for encoding information in a digital square wave by varying the fraction of time the signal is HIGH. The frequency stays fixed. The duty cycle changes.

- Cycle time ($T$)  
  The total duration of 1 complete period.
- Duty time ($t_{on}$)  
  The portion of each cycle during which the signal is HIGH.

$$
\text{Duty Cycle} = \frac{t_{on}}{T}
$$

Here:

- $t_{on}$: duty time — time the signal is HIGH per period
- $T$: cycle time — total period length

The time-averaged voltage of the signal is:

$$
\bar{V} = V_{supply} \times \text{Duty Cycle}
$$

Hardware implementation: an MCU timer increments a counter at a fixed clock rate. A compare register holds the duty cycle value. The output pin goes HIGH when the counter resets and LOW when the counter matches the compare register.

Resolution is determined by the counter width: an $n$-bit timer provides $2^n$ duty cycle steps.

Applications:

- Motor speed control  
  Average voltage controls rotational speed.
- LED brightness  
  Human eye integrates the flicker; perceived brightness tracks duty cycle.
- Servo position  
  Pulse width (typically 1–2 ms within a 20 ms period) encodes angle.
- Buzzer tone  
  PWM frequency sets the audible pitch.

## Simulated Analog Output

PWM can approximate a true analog voltage without a DAC. Passing the PWM signal through a low-pass RC filter removes the switching component, leaving a DC level equal to $\bar{V}$.

Filter cutoff must be much lower than the PWM frequency to reduce ripple. A higher PWM frequency reduces ripple for the same filter. Output accuracy is limited by PWM resolution and filter component tolerances.
